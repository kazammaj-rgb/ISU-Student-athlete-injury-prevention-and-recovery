A. Heat, sun exposure, and hydration problems

1. Dehydration
What it is
Dehydration means that the body has lost more fluid than it has replaced. During exercise, the body produces heat and releases sweat to help remove that heat. If fluid losses are not adequately replaced, dehydration can develop.
It can affect concentration, physical performance, and tolerance of heat. It may also occur alongside another condition, such as gastroenteritis or heat exhaustion. Dehydration and heat exhaustion are related, but they are not interchangeable diagnoses. NHS
Main causes and why it happens during a meet
The main sports-related cause is fluid loss through sweating without adequate replacement. Contributing circumstances include long events, several games in one day, limited access to drinks, and beginning competition with an existing fluid deficit.
Vomiting, diarrhea, fever, and medicines that increase urination can increase the risk. An athlete who has been ill before the meet may therefore become dehydrated more easily than teammates doing the same activity. NHS
Symptoms to recognize
Possible symptoms include thirst, dry mouth, headache, fatigue, dizziness, reduced urination, and darker urine. Persistent dizziness on standing or an unusually rapid heartbeat warrants medical assessment. Symptoms alone cannot establish the severity or exclude another cause.
Urine color is a useful clue, but it should not be the only assessment. Recent drinking, diet, medicines, and exercise can affect it. NHS
First aid and treatment
1. Stop the athlete’s activity and move them to a cooler place.
2. Check alertness, breathing, and whether they can swallow normally.
3. If fully alert, offer small, repeated sips rather than forcing a large drink.
4. For mild sweat-related fluid loss, water alongside normal food may be sufficient.
5. Following prolonged heavy sweating, an appropriate electrolyte-containing drink may help replace fluid and sodium.
6. Arrange medical assessment if the athlete cannot keep fluids down, has persistent symptoms, or passes very little urine.
Severe dehydration requires clinical assessment and may need intravenous fluid treatment. IV fluids must be prescribed and administered by qualified personnel. NHS
Appropriate medicine or product
There is no tablet that replaces missing body fluid.
Oral rehydration solution—ORS—is particularly useful when dehydration involves diarrhea or vomiting. A commercial sachet must be mixed with exactly the volume of safe water stated on its label. Do not make the mixture stronger or add extra salt.
A sports drink and ORS are different products. Neither should be consumed in unlimited quantities, and neither replaces assessment when the athlete is seriously unwell. NHS
Prevention and recovery
Provide accessible water and drinking opportunities before, during, and after activity. Competitive athletes benefit from an individual plan based on their event, conditions, and sweat losses.
Avoid forced overdrinking. Excessive fluid intake can cause exercise-associated hyponatremia—dangerously low blood sodium—even when the drink contains electrolytes. When an individual plan is unavailable, drinking according to thirst is a practical way to reduce overdrinking risk. Return to activity should follow symptom resolution and reassessment, rather than simply finishing a bottle of water. pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov

2. Heat cramps
What they are
Heat cramps are painful, involuntary muscle contractions associated with exertion in hot conditions. The calves, thighs, and abdominal muscles may be affected.
The broader term exercise-associated muscle cramps is often more accurate because cramps can occur without excessive heat. They may develop during exercise or after it ends. PMC
Main causes and why they happen
There is no single explanation for every athlete. Muscle fatigue, altered neuromuscular control, and—particularly in athletes with substantial sweat losses—fluid and electrolyte disturbances can contribute.
Therefore, a cramp does not automatically mean “low potassium,” and it does not automatically prove dehydration. The circumstances and accompanying symptoms matter. PMC
Symptoms and important distinctions
A typical cramp causes a sudden tightening or knot in a muscle. The athlete may temporarily struggle to move normally.
Pain after a collision, a tearing sensation, substantial bruising, or persistent loss of function may indicate an injury rather than a simple cramp. Severe muscle pain, unusual weakness, or tea- or cola-colored urine requires urgent assessment for rhabdomyolysis, a potentially serious muscle breakdown condition. www.niams.nih.gov
First aid and treatment
1. Stop the activity.
2. Rest in shade or a cool room.
3. Gently stretch the affected muscle; do not bounce or force the movement.
4. Gentle massage may help when there is no suspected tear or injury.
5. If the athlete is alert, offer appropriate fluids.
6. After substantial sweating, a sodium-containing drink or ordinary food containing some salt may be useful.
Do not immediately send the athlete back into competition because the muscle briefly relaxes. Observe for recurrence and other symptoms. CDC Yellow Book™
Appropriate medicine or product
Routine cramps usually do not need medicine. Painkillers do not address the cause and should not be used to make continued participation possible.
Avoid self-prescribed salt tablets. They can cause gastrointestinal problems, and extra salt is not appropriate for every person. Seek medical advice for cramps that last longer than an hour, repeatedly recur, or occur in someone with heart disease or a prescribed low-sodium diet. www.cdc.gov
Prevention
Increase workload gradually, build sport-specific conditioning, and allow recovery between demanding sessions. Hydration and nutrition should match the athlete’s needs.
Water, electrolyte drinks, or a particular food cannot guarantee freedom from cramps. Prevention must also address fatigue, training load, and previous episodes. pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov

3. Heat exhaustion
What it is
Heat exhaustion occurs when heat stress produces substantial weakness and an inability to continue exercise safely. It commonly involves heavy sweating, circulatory strain, and fluid loss.
It can develop during one demanding event or after repeated exposure across several hot days. It requires prompt attention because the symptoms can overlap with more serious conditions. CDC Yellow Book™
Main causes and contributing factors
Risk increases with strenuous activity, hot and humid conditions, inadequate recovery, excessive clothing or protective equipment, and insufficient adaptation to exercising in the heat.
Humidity makes sweat evaporation less effective. Consequently, sweating heavily does not necessarily mean the body is cooling adequately. Dehydration increases risk, but drinking enough does not make an athlete immune to heat illness. CDC Yellow Book™
Symptoms to recognize
Common symptoms include:
* Marked tiredness or weakness.
* Dizziness or feeling faint.
* Headache.
* Nausea or vomiting.
* Heavy sweating.
* Thirst.
* Muscle cramps.
* Reduced ability to continue activity.
Confusion, abnormal behavior, impaired coordination, seizures, or reduced consciousness require emergency management. Do not label these symptoms as ordinary tiredness. CDC
First aid and treatment
1. Remove the athlete from participation and contact the medical team.
2. Move them into shade or air conditioning.
3. Remove unnecessary equipment and loosen clothing.
4. Have them lie down; elevate the legs if comfortable and there is no suspected injury.
5. Wet the skin with cool water and fan continuously.
6. Add cloth-wrapped cold packs around the neck and armpits.
7. Offer small sips of cool water or an appropriate electrolyte drink only if fully alert and swallowing normally.
8. Stay with the athlete and reassess their condition.
They should begin improving with cooling and rest. If there is no clear improvement within approximately 30 minutes, obtain urgent medical help. Escalate immediately for worsening symptoms or emergency warning signs.www.nata.org
Appropriate medicine
Cooling and appropriate fluid replacement are the treatments.
Paracetamol, ibuprofen, and aspirin do not correct exercise-related overheating. Fever medicines should not be used to treat suspected heatstroke. Hospital treatment may include laboratory tests and carefully monitored fluids when clinically indicated. sccm.org
Recovery and prevention
An athlete with heat exhaustion should not return to sport that day. Return requires recovery, adequate hydration, and appropriate medical guidance.
Prevention includes gradual heat adaptation, reduced workload when conditions are unsafe, cooling breaks, and avoiding pressure to continue despite symptoms. Being physically fit does not eliminate the risk. PMC

4. Heat syncope
What it is
Heat syncope is a brief faint associated with heat exposure. It often occurs during prolonged standing or after a change in posture.
Heat causes blood vessels near the skin to widen. Blood can pool in the legs, temporarily reducing the blood returning to the heart and reaching the brain. Dehydration and inadequate heat adaptation may contribute. CDC Yellow Book™
Why it may happen at a regional meet
Possible situations include standing through a long opening ceremony, waiting motionless in the sun, or standing suddenly after resting.
However, collapse during active exercise must not automatically be diagnosed as heat syncope. A heart problem, heatstroke, low blood glucose, or another serious condition may be responsible. www.cdc.gov
Symptoms
The person may feel lightheaded, nauseated, sweaty, or notice dimming vision before briefly losing consciousness. In an uncomplicated faint, recovery is usually prompt after lying down.
Persistent confusion, breathing problems, chest pain, or incomplete recovery are warning signs that the event may not be a simple faint. NHS
First aid
1. If the person feels faint, help them lie down before they fall.
2. If already collapsed, check responsiveness and normal breathing.
3. If unresponsive and not breathing normally, activate emergency help, start CPR, and use an AED as soon as available.
4. If breathing normally, protect the airway and follow the event medical team’s or emergency dispatcher’s instructions.
5. For an alert person without suspected injury, lying flat with the legs raised can help.
6. Move into a cooler environment when safe.
7. Offer small sips only after full alertness and safe swallowing return.
8. Do not allow an immediate return to standing or competition. NHS
Appropriate medicine and medical review
Simple heat syncope normally needs no medicine. Positioning, cooling, appropriate fluids, and evaluation of the cause are the priorities.
Get emergency help for fainting during exercise, associated chest pain or palpitations, serious injury, or failure to recover promptly. Even after recovery, a student-athlete should be medically assessed before returning to competition. NHS
Prevention
Avoid prolonged motionless standing, allow seated or shaded breaks, move the legs while waiting, and stand up gradually. Maintain appropriate hydration and adapt progressively to exercising in hot conditions. These steps reduce risk but do not exclude an underlying medical cause. www.cdc.gov

5. Heat rash or prickly heat
What it is
Heat rash, medically called miliaria, develops when sweat ducts become blocked and sweat is trapped in the skin.
It may cause small raised bumps, a prickling sensation, itching, or mild swelling. It is not contagious. Skin changes may be less visibly red on darker skin. NHS
Main causes and why it happens
Hot, humid environments, heavy sweating, close-fitting clothing, and skin occlusion contribute. Athletes may notice it beneath uniforms or equipment and in areas such as the neck, trunk, groin, or skin folds.
Continued sweating and rubbing can make the discomfort worse. Not every sweaty-area rash is miliaria; fungal infection, contact dermatitis, and folliculitis can look similar. DermNet NZ
First aid and treatment
1. Reduce heat exposure and strenuous activity temporarily.
2. Change out of wet or tight clothing.
3. Cool the skin with a shower or cool damp cloth.
4. Dry gently and keep the area ventilated.
5. Avoid scratching, fragranced products, and unnecessary rubbing.
Most uncomplicated cases improve after the skin remains cooler and sweating decreases, often within a few days. Persistent or worsening rash needs reassessment. NHS
Appropriate medicine or product
A pharmacist may recommend:
* Calamine lotion to soothe itching.
* An age-appropriate oral antihistamine when itching warrants it.
* A short course of mild topical hydrocortisone in selected cases.
These products relieve symptoms; they do not replace cooling. Ask about drowsiness before using an antihistamine in an athlete.
Children—especially those younger than 10—need professional advice before hydrocortisone use. Do not apply it to the face, genital area, broken skin, or suspected infection without appropriate advice. Antibiotics are not routine treatment for uncomplicated heat rash. NHS
When to seek medical care
Seek assessment if the rash fails to improve, becomes painful, develops pus, or is accompanied by fever or spreading redness. These findings may suggest infection or a different diagnosis. NHS
Prevention
Use breathable clothing, change out of sweaty uniforms, shower after activity, and allow skin to dry. Avoid heavy, occlusive products over areas prone to the rash. Cooling breaks help reduce continuous sweating. DermNet NZ

6. Sunburn
What it is
Sunburn is skin injury caused by excessive ultraviolet radiation. It can occur even when the air feels cool or the sky is cloudy.
All skin tones can be affected. Symptoms include painful or tender skin, warmth, swelling, and later peeling. Redness may be less obvious on brown or black skin. More severe injury can produce blisters. NHS
Main causes and why it happens
The main cause is UV exposure beyond what the skin can tolerate. During a meet, risk increases when athletes spend long periods outdoors without enough shade, protective clothing, or correctly applied sunscreen.
Sweating, swimming, towel drying, and rubbing can reduce sunscreen protection. A “water-resistant” label does not mean protection lasts indefinitely. www.aad.org
First aid and treatment
1. Leave direct sunlight.
2. Cool the skin with a cool shower or damp cloth.
3. Apply a gentle, unperfumed moisturizer; an aloe-containing moisturizer may soothe discomfort.
4. Maintain appropriate hydration.
5. Wear loose clothing and protect affected areas from further sun exposure.
6. Leave blisters intact and keep them clean.
7. Avoid picking or peeling damaged skin.
Do not apply ice directly to sunburned skin. Extensive blistering or systemic symptoms require medical assessment. Mild sunburn often improves over about a week, but deeper injury may take longer. NHS
Appropriate medicine
Paracetamol may relieve sunburn pain when suitable. Ibuprofen may also relieve pain and inflammation, but it is unsuitable for some people and should be avoided when dehydration or kidney-related risk is present.
Before giving either medicine to a student, check the product’s age and weight instructions, previous doses, allergies, and medical conditions. Check combination cold or flu products to avoid accidentally repeating the same ingredient.
Do not give aspirin to children under 16 unless specifically prescribed. Pain relief does not reverse UV damage or make additional exposure safe. NHS
When to seek medical help
Get prompt assessment for extensive blistering, substantial swelling, fever, chills, vomiting, marked dizziness, or signs of infection. Confusion or collapse requires emergency care rather than treatment as sunburn alone. NHS
Prevention
Use broad-spectrum, water-resistant SPF 30 or higher sunscreen on exposed skin. Apply before exposure according to the label, use enough, and cover commonly missed areas such as the ears, neck, and tops of the feet.
Reapply at least every two hours and after swimming, heavy sweating, or towel drying. Combine sunscreen with shade, suitable clothing, a hat when practical, and UV-protective eyewear. Repeated sunburn increases long-term skin damage and skin cancer risk. aad.org

B. Respiratory illnesses, allergies, and exercise-related breathing conditions

1. Common cold or acute viral upper respiratory infection
What the condition is
The common cold is an infection mainly affecting the nose and throat. Several viruses can cause it. The body’s inflammatory response produces congestion, discharge, irritation, and cough.
A cold is usually self-limiting, meaning the immune system clears it without a medicine that specifically eliminates the virus. However, “cold-like symptoms” can also occur with influenza, COVID-19, or allergies.
Main causes and why it happens during a meet
An athlete acquires a respiratory virus from another person. Shared transport, crowded accommodation, close conversations, and poorly ventilated rooms can increase opportunities for exposure. The sport itself does not create the infection.
Symptoms may begin before arrival or during the meet, depending on when exposure occurred. CDC
Common symptoms
Typical complaints include a runny or blocked nose, sneezing, sore throat, mild cough, headache, and tiredness. Significant breathlessness, persistent chest pain, or marked deterioration should not be dismissed as an ordinary cold.
First aid and treatment
1. Reduce strenuous activity if the athlete feels unwell.
2. Provide rest and normal fluid intake.
3. Use saline nasal spray or drops for congestion.
4. Honey may soothe cough in anyone older than one year.
5. Reduce close contact with teammates while symptomatic.
Treatment mainly relieves discomfort while the illness resolves.
Medicines and precautions
Paracetamol may relieve pain or fever when appropriate. A pharmacist can assess whether other symptom medicines are suitable.
Antibiotics do not cure a viral cold. Avoid combining several cold products: they may contain the same painkiller or other ingredients. Cough and cold medicines have age restrictions and are not recommended for children younger than six under CDC guidance. CDC
Prevention, medical review, and return to sport
Improve ventilation, clean hands, cover coughs, and avoid sharing bottles. Seek assessment for breathing difficulty, dehydration, prolonged fever, symptoms that persist without improvement, or improvement followed by deterioration.
Resume exercise gradually when recovering. Fever medicine should not be used to conceal illness so a student can compete. www.cdc.gov

2. Influenza
What the condition is
Influenza is an acute respiratory infection caused by influenza viruses. It often produces a more abrupt and physically demanding illness than an ordinary cold.
It can affect otherwise healthy athletes, and complications can include pneumonia or worsening of an existing condition such as asthma.
Main causes and why it happens
Influenza spreads between people, particularly during close contact. Indoor team gatherings, travel, and shared accommodation can facilitate transmission.
Typical illness begins suddenly with fever, chills, headache, muscle or joint aches, cough, sore throat, and pronounced fatigue. Not every infected person develops fever. Symptoms alone cannot reliably distinguish influenza from COVID-19. who.int
First aid and treatment
1. Remove the athlete from competition if feverish or significantly unwell.
2. Arrange rest and appropriate hydration.
3. Monitor breathing, alertness, and ability to drink.
4. Reduce exposure to teammates.
5. Seek early medical advice if the athlete has asthma, another significant condition, or severe symptoms.
Medicines and precautions
A clinician may prescribe an antiviral such as oseltamivir, depending on the patient and local guidance. Treatment is most effective when started early, ideally within 48 hours.
Later treatment can still help patients with severe illness or increased complication risk. Therefore, someone who presents after two days should not assume that assessment is pointless.
Paracetamol may relieve fever and aches when suitable. Antibiotics do not treat influenza itself. Do not give aspirin to children or teenagers with a suspected viral illness unless specifically directed by a clinician. CDC
Prevention and recovery
Vaccination according to current local recommendations reduces relevant influenza risks. Cleaner air, respiratory hygiene, and avoiding close contact while ill also help.
Return only after meaningful recovery, with gradual increases in effort. An athlete should not compete with fever, marked weakness, dehydration, or chest symptoms. New chest pain, palpitations, faintness, or unusual breathlessness requires assessment. who.int

3. COVID-19
What the condition is
COVID-19 is caused by SARS-CoV-2. It can affect the nose, throat, lungs, and sometimes other body systems.
Possible symptoms include sore throat, cough, fever, fatigue, headache, muscle aches, congestion, and changes in taste or smell. Some people develop gastrointestinal symptoms or significant breathing problems.
Main causes and why it happens
Infection occurs through exposure to the virus, particularly by inhaling infectious respiratory particles. Close contact and inadequate ventilation can increase exposure.
A student may arrive with mild symptoms that are mistaken for allergy or a cold. Testing and clinical assessment may be needed, especially when the result affects treatment or outbreak management. CDC
First aid and treatment
1. Stop strenuous activity when significantly unwell.
2. Reduce exposure to other participants.
3. Arrange testing according to clinical and local guidance.
4. Maintain rest, fluids, and monitoring.
5. Obtain prompt advice for higher-risk athletes, even if initial symptoms are mild.
Severe breathing difficulty, confusion, or persistent chest pain requires emergency assessment.
Medicines and precautions
Most mild cases receive supportive care. Suitable pain or fever medicine may reduce discomfort.
Selected higher-risk patients may qualify for prescription antivirals, such as nirmatrelvir/ritonavir or remdesivir. Treatment generally needs to begin within five to seven days, depending on the medicine.
Eligibility depends on factors including age, weight, medical risk, kidney function, drug interactions, and local availability. These medicines should not be recommended automatically to every student with a positive test. Antibiotics do not treat SARS-CoV-2 itself. CDC
Prevention and return to sport
Follow current Philippine public-health and event requirements. Vaccination where recommended, cleaner air, hygiene, and precautions around symptomatic people reduce risk.
Return progressively after recovery. Persistent or new chest pain, palpitations, fainting, or disproportionate breathlessness needs medical review. If exercise causes a delayed worsening of persistent post-COVID symptoms, seek individualized advice rather than forcing a standard training progression. www.cdc.gov

4. Acute pharyngitis
What the condition is
Pharyngitis means inflammation of the pharynx, the back of the throat. It is a description of the affected area, not proof of a particular organism.
Viruses cause many cases. Group A Streptococcus is an important bacterial cause because appropriate antibiotic treatment reduces transmission and certain complications.
Main causes and symptoms
Infectious organisms spread between people. Symptoms include throat pain, painful swallowing, fever, and tender neck glands. Cough, nasal discharge, or hoarseness may accompany viral illness.
When clear viral features are absent, examination alone cannot reliably distinguish streptococcal from viral infection. A rapid antigen test or throat culture may be appropriate. www.cdc.gov
First aid and treatment
1. Stop activity if fever, weakness, or dehydration is present.
2. Offer comfortable-temperature drinks and soft food.
3. Encourage rest.
4. Arrange assessment if swallowing is difficult or symptoms are significant.
5. Use age-appropriate pain relief when suitable.
Lozenges can offer temporary relief for someone old enough to use them safely. Avoid anything that presents a choking risk.
Medicines and precautions
Paracetamol may relieve pain or fever. Ibuprofen is an alternative for some patients, but suitability depends on hydration and medical history.
Confirmed group A streptococcal infection normally requires prescribed antibiotics. Penicillin or amoxicillin is commonly used when appropriate. The clinician selects the dose, duration, and allergy alternative.
Do not use leftover antibiotics, share prescriptions, or stop treatment differently from the prescriber’s instructions. NHS
Prevention, warning signs, and return to sport
Avoid shared bottles and utensils and practise respiratory hygiene.
Drooling, inability to swallow, breathing difficulty, severe neck swelling, or rapid worsening needs urgent assessment. An athlete treated for strep throat must follow the clinician’s advice on infectiousness as well as recover physically before returning. Being less contagious does not automatically mean being ready to compete. www.cdc.gov

5. Tonsillitis
What the condition is
Tonsillitis is inflammation or infection of the tonsils, which sit on either side of the back of the throat. It can occur alongside pharyngitis, so the two entries overlap.
Most cases are viral, although bacterial infection can occur.
Causes and symptoms
The infections responsible may spread between people. Symptoms can include sore throat, fever, painful swallowing, swollen tonsils, tender neck glands, earache, and fatigue.
White patches may appear on the tonsils, but white patches alone do not establish that antibiotics are required. The cause and clinical severity need assessment.
First aid and supportive care
1. Rest and avoid strenuous activity when unwell.
2. Offer cool or comfortably warm drinks.
3. Use soft foods if tolerated.
4. Monitor fluid intake and urination.
5. Arrange assessment if pain prevents adequate drinking.
Paracetamol or, when suitable, ibuprofen may relieve discomfort. A pharmacist can advise about age-appropriate lozenges. NHS
Treatment and medicines
Viral tonsillitis often improves with supportive treatment. A clinician may prescribe antibiotics for an appropriate bacterial diagnosis.
Persistent severe symptoms may require testing for another cause, including infectious mononucleosis. If mononucleosis is suspected, sport participation needs specific clinical advice because complications can affect exercise safety.
Emergency warning signs
A peritonsillar abscess, also called quinsy, can develop. Seek urgent care for severe one-sided pain, a muffled voice, difficulty opening the mouth, drooling, or inability to swallow. Breathing difficulty requires emergency help. www.nhs.uk
Prevention and return to sport
Reduce infection exposure through hygiene and avoiding shared drinks. A student should regain comfortable swallowing, adequate hydration, and usual energy before resuming strenuous activity.
Recurrent episodes deserve a planned medical review. Do not assume that repeated antibiotic courses or tonsil surgery are automatically necessary; those decisions require assessment.

6. Acute sinusitis
What the condition is
Acute sinusitis is inflammation of the sinus lining. The sinuses are air-filled spaces connected to the nasal passages.
A viral cold commonly starts the process. Swelling interferes with drainage, leading to congestion, pressure, and discomfort.
Causes and symptoms
Symptoms include blocked or runny nose, reduced smell, facial tenderness, headache, tooth discomfort, cough, and sometimes fever.
Yellow or green discharge alone does not prove bacterial infection. Most early cases do not require antibiotics. Symptoms that persist, substantially worsen, or occur with severe systemic illness need reassessment. NHS
First aid and treatment
1. Rest when unwell.
2. Maintain normal hydration.
3. Consider a commercial saline nasal spray.
4. Avoid smoke and known allergy triggers.
5. Use appropriate pain relief if needed.
For nasal irrigation, use a suitable prepared solution and distilled, sterile, or previously boiled and cooled water. Keep the device clean.
Medicines and precautions
A clinician may recommend a nasal corticosteroid for selected patients. An antihistamine may help when allergy contributes.
Antibiotics are reserved for situations in which the clinical assessment supports their use. Do not self-select an antibiotic based only on mucus color or facial pressure.
Decongestant sprays are only for short-term use according to the product and professional advice; prolonged use can worsen congestion. www.nhs.uk
When to seek urgent care
Eye swelling, double vision, reduced vision, severe frontal headache, swelling over the forehead, neck stiffness, or neurological symptoms can indicate complications and require urgent hospital assessment. NICE
Prevention and return to sport
Manage established nasal allergy, avoid smoking and vaping, and reduce respiratory infection exposure. Resume training gradually when fever and substantial discomfort have resolved. Persistent or recurrent symptoms need clinical review rather than repeated self-treatment.

7. Acute bronchitis
What the condition is
Acute bronchitis is inflammation of the larger airways carrying air into the lungs. It is commonly caused by respiratory viruses.
Inflammation stimulates coughing and may increase mucus production. The cough can remain after the initial infection begins to settle.
Causes and symptoms
It may follow a cold, influenza, or another respiratory infection. Symptoms include cough, mucus, throat irritation, fatigue, and chest discomfort from coughing.
Smoke and other irritants can aggravate symptoms. Wheezing or shortness of breath should prompt assessment for asthma or another condition rather than automatically being attributed to bronchitis. Health
First aid and treatment
1. Stop strenuous activity if breathless, feverish, or significantly fatigued.
2. Rest and maintain appropriate fluids.
3. Avoid smoke and vaping.
4. Consider honey for cough in someone older than one year.
5. Arrange assessment for concerning symptoms.
Cough often lasts around three weeks, but duration varies.
Medicines and precautions
Paracetamol may relieve pain or fever when appropriate. Antibiotics are not routine treatment for uncomplicated acute viral bronchitis.
A cough medicine does not necessarily shorten the illness, and some cause drowsiness or contain multiple ingredients. A pharmacist should assess suitability.
Bronchodilator inhalers or oral steroids should not automatically be given to everyone with bronchitis. They may be appropriate for a separate indication, such as an asthma exacerbation. NHS
Warning signs and recovery
Seek medical assessment for coughing blood, chest pain, breathlessness, persistent or worsening fever, or failure to improve. A cough lasting more than three weeks needs review, especially if accompanied by weight loss or other concerning symptoms.
Pneumonia and other illnesses may initially resemble bronchitis. Return to sport should be gradual and should stop if coughing, breathlessness, or fatigue becomes disproportionate to the effort. NHS

8. Pneumonia
What the condition is
Pneumonia is an infection affecting lung tissue. Inflammation can interfere with normal gas exchange, making breathing and physical exertion more difficult.
Bacteria and viruses are important causes. Pneumonia is not an ordinary minor sideline complaint, and suspected cases require clinical assessment.
Causes and symptoms
It can occur as a primary infection or follow another respiratory illness. Symptoms may include fever, cough, rapid or difficult breathing, chest pain, fatigue, and reduced appetite.
A patient does not need to have every symptom. The amount or color of mucus alone cannot confirm or exclude pneumonia.
First aid
1. Remove the athlete from activity.
2. Let them rest in a comfortable position.
3. Summon the medical team.
4. Arrange prompt assessment rather than waiting for the next event.
5. Activate emergency care for severe breathing difficulty, confusion, or blue or grey lips.
A clinician may check oxygen saturation, examine the chest, and decide whether imaging or laboratory tests are needed. NHS
Treatment and medicines
Bacterial pneumonia generally requires prescribed antibiotics. The choice depends on age, severity, allergies, suspected organisms, and local practice.
Viral pneumonia does not automatically benefit from antibiotics, although bacterial coinfection may require them. Some patients need hospital treatment, oxygen, or other monitored care.
Pain or fever medicine can improve comfort but does not replace treatment of the underlying illness. Seek reassessment if the prescribed treatment is not producing the expected improvement. www.nhs.uk
Prevention and return to sport
Recommended vaccination, respiratory hygiene, and avoiding smoke help reduce relevant risks.
Recovery often takes weeks. Return should be individualized, particularly after hospitalization or significant breathing impairment. Begin with light activity only after clinical improvement and increase gradually. Fever, persistent chest pain, unusual breathlessness, or substantial fatigue requires reassessment before hard training. www.cdc.gov

9. Allergic rhinitis
What the condition is
Allergic rhinitis is an allergic inflammation of the nasal lining. It is not an infection and is not contagious.
The immune system reacts to an allergen and releases inflammatory chemicals. Symptoms can affect breathing comfort, sleep, concentration, and participation.
Causes and symptoms
Triggers include pollen, dust mites, mould, and animal dander. Dusty accommodation or exposure to an athlete’s particular allergen can aggravate symptoms during a meet.
Typical symptoms are sneezing, nasal itching, watery discharge, congestion, and itchy or watery eyes. Fever or pronounced body aches should not automatically be explained by allergy. NHS
Immediate care and treatment
Move away from the suspected trigger where practical. Saline nasal spray may help remove secretions and irritants.
Treatment may include:
* A less-sedating oral antihistamine, such as an age-appropriate loratadine or cetirizine product.
* A nasal corticosteroid, such as a suitable fluticasone or budesonide product.
* A nasal antihistamine or combination product when clinically appropriate.
Persistent nasal symptoms often respond well to correctly used nasal corticosteroids. Treatment choice should match severity and the athlete’s age.
Medicine precautions
Antihistamines can cause drowsiness, including in some people taking products described as non-drowsy. Avoid discovering this effect for the first time immediately before competition.
Decongestants can have unwanted effects, and prolonged nasal decongestant use can worsen blockage. Antibiotics do not treat allergic rhinitis. aaaai.org
Prevention and return to sport
Identify specific triggers rather than avoiding all outdoor activity. Keep accommodation clean, manage dampness, and follow the established treatment plan.
Well-controlled rhinitis usually does not prevent sport. New wheeze, chest tightness, or shortness of breath needs assessment for coexisting asthma. Throat swelling or rapidly worsening breathing is an emergency, not ordinary nasal allergy. entnet.org

10. Asthma flare-up
What the condition is
Asthma is a chronic condition involving airway inflammation and variable narrowing of the lower airways. A flare-up is a worsening that can become life-threatening.
Airway muscles tighten, the lining swells, and mucus may increase. This reduces airflow.
Causes and symptoms
Triggers include viral infections, allergens, smoke, pollution, cold air, exercise, and inadequate preventive treatment.
Symptoms include cough, wheezing, chest tightness, and breathlessness. Increasing reliever use or symptoms disturbing sleep suggest inadequate control.
Severe asthma may produce little audible wheeze because airflow is very limited. A quiet chest in a visibly distressed athlete is concerning. NHS
First aid
1. Stop activity and help the athlete sit upright.
2. Stay with them and call the event medical team.
3. Help them use their prescribed reliever according to their written asthma action plan.
4. Use a spacer with a compatible metered-dose inhaler when appropriate.
5. Activate emergency help immediately for severe symptoms, deterioration, or inadequate response.
6. If the athlete has no available reliever and is having an attack, obtain emergency help rather than waiting.
Do not make them lie flat or continue exercising.
Treatment and medicines
The reliever depends on the prescribed regimen. It may be an appropriate ICS–formoterol inhaler or salbutamol within an ICS-containing treatment plan. Not every combination inhaler can be used as a reliever.
Current GINA guidance advises against treating asthma with a short-acting reliever alone as the entire regimen. Severe attacks may require additional bronchodilators, oxygen, and systemic corticosteroids under clinical supervision. NHS
Prevention and return to sport
Review technique, adherence, triggers, and access to medicines. Keep a current action plan with the athlete and relevant staff.
After an attack, arrange prompt medical follow-up even if symptoms improve. Return requires adequate control and individualized advice. Asthma can often be managed well enough for full participation; repeated attacks should trigger treatment review rather than acceptance as normal. NHS

11. Exercise-induced bronchoconstriction—EIB
What the condition is
EIB is temporary narrowing of the lower airways associated with exercise. It can occur in people with asthma and in some people without previously diagnosed asthma.
The term does not mean that exercise itself created chronic asthma.
Causes and symptoms
Rapid ventilation during exertion increases airway water loss. In susceptible airways, this contributes to the release of mediators that cause narrowing.
Cold or dry air and airborne irritants may increase symptoms. Complaints include cough, wheeze, chest tightness, or exercise limitation during or after activity.
Breathlessness alone does not establish EIB. Diagnosis requires objective assessment, often lung-function measurements before and after a suitable exercise or airway challenge. thoracic.org
First aid
1. Stop the activity.
2. Allow a comfortable upright position.
3. Follow the athlete’s prescribed action plan.
4. Use the prescribed reliever as directed.
5. Obtain urgent help for severe or persistent symptoms.
A first unexplained episode should be assessed rather than treated indefinitely with a borrowed inhaler.
Treatment and medicines
A clinician may prescribe a pre-exercise inhaled medicine, such as salbutamol, or use an appropriate ICS-containing strategy when asthma is present.
Frequent symptoms despite pretreatment require reassessment. The problem may involve poor inhaler technique, inadequate control of airway inflammation, an incorrect diagnosis, or coexisting EILO.
Do not keep increasing medication without review. academic.oup.com
Prevention and return to sport
Use a structured warm-up, reduce relevant irritant exposure, and keep prescribed treatment available. Where cold air is a trigger, an appropriate heat-and-moisture-conserving face covering may help during suitable activities.
Confirmed and controlled EIB is compatible with sport participation. Recurring attacks, reduced performance despite treatment, or reliance on frequent rescue doses requires review before continued hard competition. thoracic.org

12. Exercise-induced laryngeal obstruction—EILO
What the condition is
EILO is temporary narrowing at the larynx, or voice box, during exercise. The narrowing can involve the vocal folds or structures above them.
It affects the upper airway, whereas EIB affects the lower airways. The two can coexist.
Causes and symptoms
During intense exercise, the larynx should remain sufficiently open for increased airflow. In EILO, structures narrow inappropriately.
Symptoms can include sudden noisy breathing, throat tightness, choking sensations, and difficulty breathing in. Symptoms often peak during hard exercise and settle relatively quickly after stopping.
The mechanism is not identical in every patient. Anxiety can accompany an alarming episode, but EILO should not be dismissed as “just panic.” thoracic.org
First aid
1. Stop exercise.
2. Help the athlete remain in a comfortable upright position.
3. Provide calm reassurance.
4. If already diagnosed, coach the rescue-breathing technique taught by their therapist.
5. Arrange assessment for a first episode, persistent symptoms, or uncertainty.
6. Activate emergency care for severe distress, reduced consciousness, or other warning signs.
Do not assume that every noisy breathing episode is EILO.
Diagnosis, treatment, and medicines
The preferred diagnostic assessment is often continuous laryngoscopy during exercise, which allows direct observation while symptoms occur.
Treatment usually involves speech-language therapy, laryngeal-control techniques, and individualized breathing practice. Associated nasal disease, reflux, or asthma may need separate treatment.
There is no standard tablet that cures EILO. Asthma medicines generally do not correct EILO itself, although they remain necessary for separately diagnosed asthma. thoracic.org
Prevention and return to sport
Practise the prescribed techniques before applying them during maximal effort. Progress from controlled exercise to sport-specific demands.
The aim is effective symptom control and continued participation where possible. Recurrent unexplained episodes need specialist review; they should not lead automatically to higher asthma doses or permanent exclusion from sport. doi.org

13. Breathing pattern disorder or dysfunctional breathing
What the condition is
Breathing pattern disorder involves an altered breathing pattern that produces troublesome symptoms or inefficient breathing. Examples include excessive upper-chest breathing, irregular breathing, frequent sighing, or overbreathing.
It is a real clinical problem. It can occur alone or with asthma, EILO, anxiety, and other conditions.
Causes and symptoms
Several factors may contribute, including an established inefficient pattern, previous illness, discomfort, stress, or responses to demanding exercise. There is not one cause for every athlete.
Symptoms may include breathlessness greater than expected for the effort, chest tightness, difficulty taking a satisfying breath, dizziness, tingling, and slow recovery between efforts. These symptoms are not specific, so other medical causes need consideration. brit-thoracic.org.uk
First aid
1. Stop or reduce the activity.
2. Check for emergency warning signs.
3. Provide calm support without dismissing the symptoms.
4. Allow a comfortable position with relaxed shoulders.
5. If already diagnosed, use the breathing method taught by the treating clinician.
6. Seek medical assessment if symptoms are new, severe, persistent, or different from previous episodes.
Do not repeatedly demand very deep breaths. Do not use paper-bag rebreathing, because an incorrectly identified breathing emergency may be made more dangerous.
Treatment and medicines
Treatment commonly involves a respiratory physiotherapist who assesses breathing at rest and during activity. Retraining may include gentle breathing control, reduced unnecessary upper-chest effort, pacing, posture, and progressively applying the technique during exercise.
There is no routine medicine that corrects every breathing pattern disorder. Inhalers are appropriate for a separate diagnosed indication, not merely because the athlete feels breathless. CUH
Prevention and return to sport
Practise the prescribed method regularly, then incorporate it into increasingly demanding exercise. Address identified triggers and coexisting conditions.
A normal-looking athlete can still have significant symptoms. Conversely, a previous diagnosis must not be used to explain every new episode without assessment. Return should follow functional improvement and an individualized plan. CUH


C. Digestive illnesses and exercise-related stomach complaints

1. Acute gastroenteritis

What it is

Acute gastroenteritis is an illness involving inflammation of the stomach and intestines. It commonly causes diarrhea, vomiting, nausea, and abdominal cramps.

Many cases are infectious. Viruses such as norovirus are important causes, but bacteria and parasites can also produce gastrointestinal illness. The expression “stomach flu” is misleading because influenza is a different infection.

Main causes and why it happens at sports events

Infection can spread through contaminated food or water, contaminated hands and surfaces, and contact with an infected person.

At a regional meet, shared accommodation, toilets, dining areas, and transport can provide opportunities for transmission. A participant who is already infected may become symptomatic during the event and expose others. www.cdc.gov

Common symptoms

Watery diarrhea, nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramps, reduced appetite, and sometimes fever may occur. The most immediate concern is fluid and electrolyte loss.

Dry mouth, reduced urination, persistent dizziness, unusual tiredness, or inability to drink adequately suggests the need for assessment. Blood in the stool or substantial abdominal pain should not be dismissed as ordinary gastroenteritis. www.niddk.nih.gov

Step-by-step first aid
1. Stop competition and allow supervised rest.
2. Reduce contact with other participants.
3. If fully alert, give small, frequent sips of oral rehydration solution—ORS.
4. Prepare ORS exactly as directed; do not add extra powder, sugar, or salt.
5. Once appetite returns, resume suitable normal food as tolerated.
6. Seek assessment if the athlete cannot retain fluids or shows dehydration.
Treatment and medicines

Replacing fluid and electrolytes is the priority. Prolonged fasting or a highly restrictive diet is generally unnecessary.

Antibiotics do not treat viral gastroenteritis. Antidiarrheal medicines should not be given routinely to children or used to suppress diarrhea accompanied by blood or fever. A clinician may prescribe medicine for significant vomiting after evaluating the cause. NIDDK

Prevention

Wash hands with soap and water after toilet use and before eating. Hand sanitizer alone does not work well against norovirus. Clean contaminated areas using appropriate products and procedures.

Recovery and return to sport

For suspected infectious vomiting or diarrhea, remain away from school or shared team activities until at least 48 hours after the last episode, subject to local requirements. The athlete also needs adequate hydration, normal intake, and recovered strength before strenuous participation. www.cdc.gov
2. Foodborne illness or food poisoning

What it is

Foodborne illness results from eating or drinking something containing harmful organisms, toxins, or other contaminants. It can cause gastroenteritis, so these two categories overlap.

The illness may begin within hours or after several days. The last meal eaten is not necessarily the responsible meal.

Main causes and why it happens

Risk increases with undercooked food, contaminated water or ice, poor hand hygiene, cross-contamination, and unsafe storage temperatures.

During a meet, packed lunches, large-scale catering, transport, and outdoor food service need careful handling. Food can be unsafe even when it looks and smells normal. www.cdc.gov

Common symptoms

Diarrhea, vomiting, nausea, abdominal cramps, and fever are common. Some infections can cause severe dehydration, bloody diarrhea, or other complications.

Several athletes becoming ill after a shared meal should raise concern for an outbreak. However, determining the source requires investigation.

Step-by-step first aid
1. Stop participation and check alertness and hydration.
2. Give small, frequent amounts of ORS if swallowing is safe.
3. Do not deliberately induce vomiting.
4. Notify the medical team if several people are affected.
5. Record symptom onset and relevant food and drink exposures.
6. Seek prompt care for blood in the stool, high fever, persistent vomiting, or dehydration.
Treatment and medicines

Many cases improve with supportive care, but treatment depends on the cause.

Antibiotics or antiparasitic medicines may be appropriate for selected infections. They should not be started automatically. Certain diarrheal infections require particular caution with antibiotics or medicines that slow bowel movement.

Children should not receive over-the-counter antidiarrheals without professional advice. Rehydration remains necessary even if a medicine temporarily reduces stool frequency. NIDDK

Prevention

Follow clean, separate, cook, and chill: clean hands and surfaces, separate raw ingredients from ready-to-eat food, cook thoroughly, and refrigerate promptly. Use safe water and ice.

People with vomiting or diarrhea should not prepare food for others. www.cdc.gov

Recovery and return to sport

Follow infectious-illness precautions where applicable. Return only after losses have stopped and eating, drinking, urination, and energy have recovered. Significant illness or dehydration needs medical review.
3. Exercise-associated nausea

What it is

Nausea is the unpleasant sensation that vomiting may occur. It is a symptom, not a diagnosis, and it does not always lead to vomiting.

A brief episode after unusually hard effort can occur, but repeated nausea is not something an athlete should simply learn to tolerate.

Main causes and why it happens

Strenuous exercise can alter digestive-system blood flow and stomach function. Heat stress, dehydration, poorly tolerated food, concentrated drinks, and competition anxiety may contribute.

A large meal shortly before activity can be uncomfortable, but inadequate fueling can also create problems. The solution is an individualized nutrition plan rather than avoiding food altogether.

Nausea may also result from infection, migraine, medicines, low blood glucose, or another medical condition. pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov

Common symptoms and warning signs

The athlete may report a queasy stomach, excessive saliva, loss of appetite, belching, or an urge to vomit.

Associated confusion, collapse, chest pain, severe headache, or substantial abdominal pain requires prompt assessment.

Step-by-step first aid
1. Stop or substantially reduce activity.
2. Move to a comfortable, cooler area.
3. Check alertness and ask about pain, heat exposure, and recent intake.
4. Offer small sips if fully alert and swallowing normally.
5. Avoid forcing a large drink or meal.
6. Obtain medical help if symptoms persist, recur, or worsen.
Once improving, small amounts of familiar food may be easier to tolerate than a large meal. www.nhs.uk

Treatment and medicines

Treatment should address the cause. Cooling is necessary for heat illness; an anti-nausea medicine does not correct overheating.

A clinician may prescribe an antiemetic when appropriate. Routine medication to suppress unexplained symptoms before every competition is not the first approach.

Prevention

Practise meal timing and fueling during training. Avoid unfamiliar gels, supplements, or concentrated drinks on competition day. Review repeated episodes with a clinician and sports dietitian.

Return to sport

Resume cautiously only when symptoms settle and the athlete can drink comfortably. Do not return someone with continuing weakness, dizziness, or suspected heat illness simply because they have not vomited. pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov
4. Exercise-associated vomiting

What it is

Vomiting is the forceful expulsion of stomach contents. It can occur after nausea or begin suddenly.

Vomiting is not proof of a productive workout or good effort. It requires attention to the athlete’s condition and possible cause.

Main causes and why it happens

Potential contributors include intense exertion, heat illness, dehydration, excessive fluid intake, poorly tolerated food or drinks, and infection.

Vomiting after a head impact can indicate concussion or a more serious injury. Vomiting with confusion or collapse during prolonged exercise can also signal a dangerous fluid or temperature disturbance. pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov

Symptoms and risks

Repeated vomiting can cause dehydration and electrolyte disturbances. A person with reduced consciousness is also at risk of inhaling vomit.

Blood, coffee-ground-like material, or green bile requires urgent assessment.

Step-by-step first aid
1. Stop participation.
2. Keep an alert athlete comfortably positioned, leaning forward when vomiting.
3. Do not leave someone actively vomiting flat on their back.
4. Check for head injury, severe pain, overheating, or altered behavior.
5. After vomiting settles, try small, frequent sips if swallowing is safe.
6. Use ORS when substantial gastrointestinal fluid loss is present.
7. Seek assessment if fluids cannot be retained.
If consciousness is reduced, protect the airway and follow emergency instructions. Suspected neck injury requires careful handling. www.nhsinform.scot

Treatment and medicines

A clinician may choose an antiemetic, such as ondansetron, after considering the cause, age, other medicines, and safety factors.

A medicine stopping vomiting does not establish that the athlete is safe to compete. Some patients need monitored fluid replacement or investigation.

Prevention and return to sport

Review heat exposure, workload, hydration, and food tolerance. Avoid forced overdrinking.

Return depends on the cause and recovery. Vomiting associated with concussion, heat illness, or significant dehydration requires specific clinical guidance. Infectious vomiting also requires exclusion precautions; a single clearly noninfectious episode does not automatically follow the same rules. www.niddk.nih.gov
5. Exercise-associated diarrhea or runner’s diarrhea

What it is

Runner’s diarrhea describes loose or frequent bowel movements during or shortly after running. It is more associated with endurance activity than short events, although individual patterns vary.

It is a symptom pattern—not confirmation that infection, inflammatory disease, or another medical cause is absent.

Main causes and why it happens

Potential contributors include repetitive movement, changes in intestinal blood flow and motility, stress, and food or drink intolerance.

Large amounts of caffeine, sugar alcohols, unfamiliar gels, or poorly tolerated meals may aggravate symptoms. Lactose can contribute in someone with lactose intolerance. NSAIDs, including ibuprofen and naproxen, can increase gastrointestinal complaints. sportsmedicine.mayoclinic.org

Symptoms and warning signs

Urgency, loose stool, rumbling, and cramping may occur.

Blood in stool is not a normal training effect. Diarrhea with fever, severe pain, repeated vomiting, or substantial weakness needs assessment.

Step-by-step first aid
1. Stop or reduce activity and provide toilet access.
2. Check for blood, fever, pain, and dehydration.
3. Replace fluid losses appropriately.
4. Use ORS for significant diarrheal losses.
5. Avoid further intake of a suspected triggering supplement or drink.
6. Arrange review for severe or recurring symptoms.
Treatment and medicines

There is no universal medicine for runner’s diarrhea.

Loperamide may be considered for selected adults with uncomplicated diarrhea, but should not become a routine way of forcing participation. It is inappropriate in several situations, especially bloody diarrhea or significant fever. Children need professional advice.

Antibiotics are not routine treatment. www.niddk.nih.gov

Prevention

Trial fueling during training. Adjust the timing and amount of personal triggers rather than removing whole food groups.

Some athletes tolerate less fiber immediately before an event, but chronic fiber restriction can contribute to constipation. Avoid prolonged fasting or underfueling to prevent bowel symptoms.

Return to sport

Resume when symptoms and fluid losses have settled. Recurrent episodes, symptoms occurring outside exercise, weight loss, or blood require clinical evaluation. If infection is suspected, follow infectious-diarrhea precautions. sportsmedicine.mayoclinic.org
6. Exercise-related abdominal cramping

What it is

Abdominal cramping describes a sensation, not a specific disease. Pain may arise from the digestive tract, abdominal muscles, or another organ.

A side stitch, bowel urgency, constipation, menstrual pain, muscle strain, and infection may all be described as “stomach cramps.”

Main causes and why it happens

Exercise can disturb gastrointestinal function. Poorly tolerated meals, gas, altered bowel movement, and a mismatched fueling plan may contribute.

However, pain beginning during sport can be unrelated to exercise. Appendicitis and other urgent conditions may initially be mistaken for cramps. pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov

Symptoms that help guide assessment

Ask about:
- Exact location and whether the pain moves.
- Sudden versus gradual onset.
- Constant pain versus episodes.
- Fever, vomiting, diarrhea, or urinary symptoms.
- A recent fall, collision, or unusual movement.
Severe or progressively localized pain is more concerning than brief discomfort that completely settles.

Step-by-step first aid
1. Stop activity.
2. Let the athlete adopt a comfortable position.
3. Check alertness and overall condition.
4. Ask about associated symptoms and recent intake.
5. Do not force food, fluids, stretching, or massage.
6. Obtain assessment for persistent, severe, recurrent, or localized pain.
Do not repeatedly press the abdomen or attempt to diagnose appendicitis at the sideline.

Treatment and medicines

Treatment follows the cause. An antacid, laxative, antidiarrheal, and antispasmodic address different problems and are not interchangeable.

Do not give several medicines while delaying assessment of significant unexplained pain. A suspected acute abdominal condition requires medical care, not a trial of “stomach medicine.” www.nhs.uk

Prevention and return to sport

For recurring minor episodes, review meal timing, food tolerance, hydration, workload, and bowel habits.

An athlete may resume cautiously after a clearly minor episode completely resolves. Pain returning with activity, persisting at rest, or worsening needs review. There is no single preventive diet or medicine for every abdominal cramp. pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov
7. Side stitch—exercise-related transient abdominal pain

What it is

A side stitch is temporary, localized abdominal pain associated with exercise, particularly repetitive torso movement such as running.

It often occurs near the lower ribs or side of the abdomen. Mild pain may feel pulling or aching; more intense pain can feel sharp or stabbing.

Main causes and what remains uncertain

The exact mechanism is not fully established. Irritation of the abdominal lining and stress on supporting tissues are among proposed explanations.

It should not be presented as definitively caused by a diaphragm cramp or inadequate oxygen. Larger meals and concentrated drinks shortly before activity can provoke symptoms in susceptible athletes. pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov

Step-by-step first aid
1. Slow down or stop.
2. Allow breathing to settle.
3. Try comfortable, controlled breathing.
4. Gentle pressure over the area or bending slightly forward may help.
5. Stop any maneuver that worsens pain.
6. Reassess if pain fails to settle.
These techniques are commonly reported to help, but no single method reliably works for everyone.

Treatment and medicines

A typical resolving stitch generally needs no medicine. Antibiotics, painkillers, or electrolyte tablets are not established cures.

Persistent or unusual pain requires assessment because other problems can resemble a stitch. A sports clinician may review technique, posture, equipment, conditioning, and nutrition. Sports Medicine Today

Prevention

Avoid a large meal or large drink immediately before activity. Practise suitable meal timing, use familiar foods, and take tolerable amounts of fluid rather than rapidly drinking a large volume.

Functional core conditioning and posture work may help selected athletes, although prevention evidence is limited.

Return to sport

Resume gently after complete resolution of a typical stitch. Stop again if it recurs.

Pain that remains at rest, follows trauma, progressively localizes, or accompanies fever or vomiting should not repeatedly be labeled a stitch. Seek assessment. pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov
8. Acid reflux or heartburn

What it is

Gastroesophageal reflux occurs when stomach contents move upward into the esophagus. Heartburn is a burning sensation that reflux may produce behind the breastbone.

**GERD—gastroesophageal reflux disease—**means reflux causes troublesome symptoms or complications. One episode of heartburn does not establish GERD.

Main causes and why it happens during sport

The barrier between the stomach and esophagus may allow contents to move upward. A full stomach, certain positions, and individual food triggers can contribute.

Symptoms may be noticed with running, bending, or activity soon after eating. A universal list of forbidden foods is not appropriate because tolerance differs.

Common symptoms

Heartburn, a sour taste, regurgitation, nausea, and upper abdominal discomfort may occur. Cough or throat symptoms can have many causes and do not prove reflux. www.niddk.nih.gov

First aid
1. Pause the provoking activity.
2. Remain upright rather than lying down.
3. Loosen uncomfortable clothing around the abdomen.
4. Avoid another large meal while symptomatic.
5. Obtain assessment for new or concerning chest pain.
Do not assume exercise-related chest pain is heartburn, especially with breathlessness, palpitations, faintness, or collapse.

Treatment and medicines

A clinician or pharmacist may recommend an age-appropriate antacid or alginate for occasional symptoms.

Persistent GERD may require an H2 blocker, such as famotidine, or a proton pump inhibitor, such as omeprazole. These medicines have different roles; a PPI is not an immediate rescue treatment.

Children should receive reflux medicines under clinical direction. Repeated self-treatment can delay recognition of another problem. NIDDK

Prevention and medical review

Use manageable meals, allow suitable digestion time, avoid lying down immediately afterward, and identify personal triggers. Avoid smoke exposure.

Difficulty swallowing, food sticking, weight loss, persistent vomiting, blood in vomit, or black stools requires assessment.

Return to sport

Occasional symptoms may settle with rest and adjustment. Recurrent exertional chest discomfort needs medical review before continued hard competition. NIDDK
9. Bloating and excessive gas

What it is

Bloating is a sensation of abdominal fullness or pressure. Distension is a visible increase in abdominal size.

Gas may contribute, but bloating can also occur without an unusually large amount of gas. Constipation, food intolerance, and other digestive conditions may be involved.

Main causes and why it happens

Swallowed air and microbial breakdown of food contribute to digestive gas.

Rapid eating between events, fizzy drinks, chewing gum, sugar alcohols, and large meals may worsen symptoms. Lactose can contribute in someone with lactose intolerance. Travel-related changes and constipation can also be relevant. NHS

Common symptoms

Fullness, pressure, rumbling, belching, flatulence, mild cramps, or a distended abdomen may occur.

Sudden severe pain, vomiting, or inability to pass stool and gas requires urgent assessment rather than treatment as ordinary gas.

First aid and treatment
1. Pause intense activity if uncomfortable.
2. Avoid another large meal or fizzy drink.
3. Allow toilet access and privacy.
4. Gentle walking may help if there are no warning signs.
5. Review food, drinks, and recent bowel movements.
Do not vigorously massage an unexplained painful or swollen abdomen.

Medicines and precautions

A pharmacist may suggest simeticone, although benefit varies. Lactase products may help someone with lactose intolerance. A laxative may be appropriate when constipation is the cause, but does not treat all bloating.

Do not automatically use antibiotics, “detox” preparations, or multiple supplements. Treatment should match the cause. NHS

Prevention

Eat slowly, use manageable portions, and identify consistent triggers. A symptom-and-food diary can help.

Avoid broad elimination diets without advice, particularly in growing athletes. Persistent symptoms may require medical assessment and a sports dietitian.

Return to sport

Mild, improving bloating may not prevent activity. Persistent pain, weight loss, bleeding, repeated vomiting, or significant distension requires review before continued strenuous exercise. NHS
10. Constipation

What it is

Constipation involves hard, painful, difficult, or unusually infrequent bowel movements. A feeling of incomplete emptying may also occur.

Daily stool passage is not a requirement for everyone. Consistency, discomfort, and a change from the person’s normal pattern are important.

Main causes and why it happens during a meet

Travel, unfamiliar toilets, withholding stool, altered routines, insufficient fiber or fluid, and certain medicines can contribute.

A student may repeatedly delay bowel movements to avoid missing an event or using a shared toilet. Hard stool can make defecation painful and encourage further withholding.

Being physically active does not eliminate constipation risk. www.nhs.uk

Common symptoms

Hard or lumpy stool, straining, painful bowel movements, bloating, and abdominal discomfort are common.

Children with retained stool can sometimes develop leakage of looser stool around it. This should not automatically be treated as infectious diarrhea. www.healthychildren.org

Initial care
1. Provide private, unhurried toilet access.
2. Encourage responding to the urge to pass stool.
3. Maintain regular meals and adequate fluids.
4. Include appropriate fiber, increasing gradually.
5. Encourage a relaxed toilet routine, often after meals.
6. Arrange assessment when symptoms persist or cause significant discomfort.
Severe pain, vomiting, marked swelling, or inability to pass gas requires urgent evaluation before laxatives are considered.

Treatment and medicines

A clinician may recommend a laxative. **Polyethylene glycol—PEG or macrogol—**is commonly used for pediatric constipation when appropriate.

The preparation, dose, and duration depend on the child and whether stool impaction is present. Some cases need a structured treatment plan.

Do not give a child laxatives or enemas without appropriate advice. Laxatives must not be used for weight control or competition weight cutting. NIDDK

Prevention and return to sport

Protect toilet access, avoid withholding, maintain adequate intake, and limit abrupt routine changes where possible.

Mild constipation may allow participation if the athlete is otherwise comfortable. Significant pain, recurrent problems, bleeding, or weight loss needs assessment. Treatment should restore comfortable bowel function, not produce an urgent bowel movement immediately before competition. www.nhs.uk


D. Skin irritation, friction problems, and minor surface injuries

1. Friction blisters

What the condition is

A friction blister is a fluid-filled pocket that develops after repeated rubbing or shearing damages the skin. The fluid is usually clear, although deeper injury may produce a blood-filled blister.

The skin covering the blister acts as a protective barrier. Removing it unnecessarily exposes tender tissue and increases the opportunity for contamination.

Main causes and why it happens during sport

Common causes include poorly fitting footwear, socks that retain moisture, repeated gripping, and rubbing from equipment. Heat and sweat can increase vulnerability.

During a regional meet, new shoes, unfamiliar equipment, repeated events, and prolonged walking between venues may contribute. Blisters can affect the heels, toes, soles, palms, or other areas—not only the feet. aad.org

Common symptoms

A hot or stinging spot may appear before the blister. Later, the athlete may notice localized pain, tenderness, and a raised fluid-filled area.

Increasing warmth, spreading redness or discoloration, pus, or worsening pain suggests possible infection.

Step-by-step first aid
1. Stop the rubbing activity.
2. Gently clean and dry the surrounding skin.
3. Leave an uncomplicated intact blister unbroken.
4. Cover it with a suitable protective dressing.
5. Use padding around the blister to reduce pressure without pressing directly on it.
6. If it opens, gently clean it and protect the exposed area with a non-adherent dressing.
Do not peel away the remaining blister roof. A large, very painful, bloody, or uncertain blister should be assessed rather than drained with improvised equipment. aad.org

Treatment and medicines

A hydrocolloid dressing may help selected uncomplicated blisters. It is not a substitute for assessment when infection is suspected.

Routine antibiotic creams are unnecessary for a clean, uninfected blister. Pain medicine may be considered when appropriate, but should not be used to conceal pain and continue damaging the skin.

Prevention and return to sport

Use correctly fitted shoes, moisture-managing socks, and suitable grip protection. Address a hot spot before it becomes a blister.

Return depends on whether the area can be protected without pain or altered movement. Avoid activity that continues to rub or reopen it. nhs.uk
2. Chafing

What the condition is

Chafing is irritation caused by repeated rubbing of skin against skin, clothing, or equipment. It commonly affects the inner thighs, groin, underarms, nipples, or areas beneath straps.

It can range from mild soreness to raw, cracked, or superficially bleeding skin.

Main causes and why it happens

Repeated movement, moisture, heat, rough seams, and poorly fitting clothing contribute. Sweat and damp fabric can make continued rubbing more damaging.

Examples include thigh rubbing during running, nipple irritation under a wet shirt, and soreness beneath a backpack or equipment strap. my.clevelandclinic.org

Common symptoms

Burning, stinging, tenderness, redness or discoloration, and a rubbed appearance are typical. Water or sweat may sting when it reaches damaged skin.

A spreading rash, pus, substantial swelling, or increasing pain may indicate infection or another condition.

Step-by-step first aid
1. Stop or modify the activity causing friction.
2. Remove wet or rubbing clothing.
3. Gently wash the area with water and a mild cleanser.
4. Pat dry; do not scrub.
5. Apply a thin protective layer of plain petroleum jelly when appropriate.
6. Cover raw areas with a suitable non-adherent dressing if further rubbing is possible.
Treatment and medicines

Most uncomplicated chafing improves with friction reduction and barrier protection.

Antibiotics, antifungals, and steroid creams are not automatic treatments. A persistent groin rash, for example, may involve intertrigo, fungal infection, or contact dermatitis and needs assessment.

Avoid fragranced products, harsh antiseptics, and products that sting or worsen the rash. my.clevelandclinic.org

Prevention

Choose well-fitting sports clothing with comfortable seams. Change damp clothing promptly and use a suitable anti-chafing barrier on vulnerable intact skin before activity. Test products during training rather than first using them at competition.

Recovery and return to sport

Resume when movement is comfortable and the skin can be protected. Continued rubbing over painful, raw skin delays healing. Seek review if the area worsens or does not improve after removing the cause. health.clevelandclinic.org
3. Intertrigo — irritation within skin folds

What the condition is

Intertrigo is inflammation where opposing skin surfaces meet, such as the groin, underarms, beneath the breasts, or between toes.

It is a description of inflammation in a fold—not a diagnosis of fungal infection. Infection may develop secondarily, but not every case is infected.

Main causes and why it happens

Friction, trapped moisture, heat, and limited airflow weaken the skin barrier. Sweaty uniforms or equipment can keep folds damp during a long meet.

Bacteria or yeast may multiply in damaged, moist skin. Other conditions, including eczema and psoriasis, can resemble intertrigo. DermNet

Common symptoms

Soreness, itching, burning, moist or softened skin, cracking, and discoloration may occur. Odor, discharge, or small surrounding spots can suggest secondary infection, but examination may be needed to determine the cause.

Step-by-step initial care
1. Reduce sweating and friction where possible.
2. Change out of damp clothing.
3. Gently clean the fold.
4. Dry carefully, including within the fold.
5. Use breathable clothing.
6. Seek assessment for persistent, painful, spreading, or recurrent symptoms.
The aim is to control excess moisture while avoiding harsh drying chemicals or aggressive rubbing.

Treatment and medicines

Treatment depends on the cause:
- Barrier products containing zinc oxide or petrolatum may help selected friction-related cases.
- A topical antifungal, such as clotrimazole, may be appropriate when yeast or fungal infection is identified.
- Bacterial infection may require prescribed treatment.
- A clinician may use a short course of a low-potency steroid for selected inflammatory cases.
Do not automatically use a combined steroid-antifungal-antibiotic cream. Strong steroids in skin folds can cause skin thinning and may disguise infection. my.clevelandclinic.org

Prevention and return to sport

Keep folds clean and appropriately dry, change sweaty clothing, and correct equipment that traps moisture.

Noninfected intertrigo is not contagious. Participation depends on comfort, skin integrity, and whether an infection is present. Recurrent episodes need assessment for contributing skin or medical conditions. pcds.org.uk
4. Irritant contact dermatitis

What the condition is

Irritant contact dermatitis occurs when a substance or repeated physical exposure directly damages the skin barrier. An allergy is not required.

It can develop after one strong exposure or repeated milder exposures.

Main causes and why it happens

Possible triggers include detergents, cleaning products, repeated washing, sweat, friction, and pool chemicals.

For example, repeated washing with harsh soap or remaining in chemically treated water may irritate susceptible skin. Someone with eczema may be more vulnerable because their skin barrier is already impaired. dermnetnz.org

Common symptoms

Burning, stinging, dryness, cracking, soreness, and discoloration may occur. Some cases blister.

The pattern often corresponds to exposure, but irritant and allergic dermatitis can coexist. A recurring rash under equipment should not automatically be attributed to sweat alone.

Step-by-step first aid
1. Stop contact with the suspected irritant.
2. Remove contaminated clothing or equipment.
3. Gently rinse away ordinary irritant residues.
4. Pat dry without rubbing.
5. Apply a suitable fragrance-free emollient when appropriate.
6. Avoid re-exposure while the skin recovers.
Concentrated chemical exposure, severe pain, eye involvement, or rapidly developing blistering requires urgent advice. A chemical burn should not be managed as an ordinary mild rash.

Treatment and medicines

Avoidance and barrier repair are the main treatments. A clinician may recommend a short course of an appropriate topical corticosteroid.

The strength and duration depend on age, body site, and severity. Do not use a strong steroid on the face, groin, or skin folds without professional direction.

Antibiotics are needed only for a relevant infection, not for uncomplicated irritation. NHS

Prevention and return to sport

Use gentle cleansing, rinse after relevant exposure, and follow correct chemical dilution and equipment-cleaning instructions.

Return when exposure can be controlled and movement or equipment use is comfortable. Persistent symptoms may require assessment for allergy or another diagnosis. dermnetnz.org
5. Allergic contact dermatitis

What the condition is

Allergic contact dermatitis is a delayed immune reaction to a substance touching the skin. It develops in a person who has become sensitized to that substance.

It differs from irritant dermatitis, although both can occur together.

Main causes and why it happens

Sports-related exposures include adhesives, rubber-manufacturing chemicals, metals, fragrances, preservatives, and ingredients in creams or sprays.

A rash beneath athletic tape may therefore result from allergy, irritation, or both. A product tolerated previously can still become a problem after sensitization. DermNet

Common symptoms

Itching, an eczema-like rash, swelling, scaling, or small blisters may develop hours to days after contact. The rash may extend beyond the precise contact area.

An immediate reaction to latex can involve a different allergic mechanism and may cause hives or anaphylaxis. It should not be treated as merely a delayed skin rash.

Step-by-step first aid
1. Stop using the suspected product.
2. Remove tape gently without further injuring the skin.
3. Wash off residues carefully.
4. Use a cool compress for discomfort.
5. Apply a suitable fragrance-free emollient.
6. Record the product name and ingredients for assessment.
Do not deliberately reapply a suspected allergen to “confirm” the reaction.

Treatment and medicines

A clinician may prescribe a topical corticosteroid appropriate to the body site and severity. Severe or extensive reactions need medical assessment.

Antihistamines may help some associated itching but do not remove the underlying delayed inflammation.

Patch testing can identify relevant contact allergens in recurrent or persistent cases. It is different from the skin-prick testing used for some immediate allergies. DermNet

Prevention and return to sport

Avoid the confirmed allergen, check product ingredients, and select compatible equipment or dressings. “Hypoallergenic” does not guarantee that a product is safe for every individual.

Resume when the skin is sufficiently recovered and the exposure has been removed. Repeatedly covering an allergic rash with the same tape prolongs the problem.
6. Eczema flare-up

What the condition is

Eczema describes inflammatory skin conditions. Here, a flare-up mainly refers to atopic eczema, which causes an impaired skin barrier, dryness, and itching.

It is not contagious. It is often recurrent rather than permanently cured by one treatment.

Main causes and why it happens

Genetic and immune factors contribute. Heat, sweating, friction, harsh cleansers, fragranced products, and stress can aggravate an individual’s eczema.

During a meet, a new uniform, repeated showering, an unfamiliar detergent, or prolonged sweating may trigger symptoms. Not every flare is caused by food allergy. NHS

Common symptoms

Itching, dryness, scaling, cracks, thickened skin, and discoloration may occur. In darker skin, inflammation may appear purple, grey, darker, or lighter rather than bright red.

Scratching can break the skin and increase infection risk.

Step-by-step initial care
1. Reduce overheating and rubbing.
2. Change out of sweaty clothing.
3. Rinse or wash gently using the athlete’s tolerated cleanser.
4. Pat dry.
5. Apply the established moisturizer.
6. Follow the athlete’s prescribed eczema treatment plan.
A cool compress may reduce discomfort. Avoid aggressive scrubbing.

Treatment and medicines

Regular emollients and appropriately prescribed topical corticosteroids are common treatments. Other medicines, such as tacrolimus or pimecrolimus, may be prescribed for selected patients.

Steroid strength, site, frequency, and duration must match the individual. More potent is not automatically better, but avoiding an appropriately prescribed treatment can also leave eczema poorly controlled.

Antibiotics do not treat the underlying eczema and are reserved for a relevant infection. aad.org

Urgent warning signs

Rapidly worsening painful eczema, groups of similar blisters or punched-out sores, fever, or eye-area involvement needs urgent assessment. Eczema herpeticum is a potentially serious viral complication requiring prompt treatment. BAD Patient Hub

Prevention and return to sport

Maintain skin care, identify genuine triggers, and avoid unnecessary elimination diets. Participation is often possible when eczema is controlled. Open, infected, or extensively painful skin requires assessment before contact or aquatic sport.
7. Hives or urticaria

What the condition is

Hives are raised, itchy swellings called wheals. Individual lesions commonly disappear within 24 hours, although new ones can develop elsewhere.

Hives are not contagious and do not always mean a food allergy.

Main causes and why it happens

Triggers include infections, medicines, insect stings, pressure, heat, cold, and exercise. Sometimes no trigger is identified.

An athlete may develop hives as body temperature rises. However, exercise-related hives require attention because they can occasionally accompany a systemic allergic reaction. NHS

Symptoms and warning signs

Itchy raised patches may vary in size and move around the body. Deeper swelling is called angioedema.

Throat tightness, breathing difficulty, faintness, collapse, or rapidly progressive swelling suggests an emergency. Anaphylaxis can occur without obvious hives.

Step-by-step first aid
1. Stop exercise.
2. Move away from a suspected trigger.
3. Check breathing, swallowing, and alertness.
4. Use a comfortable cool environment and a cool cloth if tolerated.
5. For isolated skin symptoms, seek pharmacist or clinician advice.
6. For suspected anaphylaxis, use prescribed adrenaline immediately and activate emergency care.
Do not wait for an antihistamine to work when airway or circulatory symptoms are present. NHS

Treatment and medicines

A clinician or pharmacist may recommend an age-appropriate, less-sedating oral antihistamine, such as loratadine or cetirizine.

Some individuals still become sleepy. Steroid creams are not the main treatment for hives, and increasing antihistamine doses beyond the label requires clinical direction.

Prevention and return to sport

Record suspected triggers and obtain assessment for repeated exercise-related episodes. An individualized emergency plan may be necessary.

Do not return to the event immediately after a suspected systemic reaction. Isolated, resolving hives also need reassessment if exercise triggered them.

Painful lesions, bruising, or individual lesions remaining fixed beyond the usual pattern require review for another diagnosis. aad.org
8. Insect bites and localized sting reactions

What the condition is

Bites and stings can cause local pain, itching, and swelling. A local reaction remains around the affected area; a systemic reaction affects other body systems.

A large local swelling does not automatically mean bacterial infection.

Main causes and why it happens

Outdoor fields, grassy areas, food waste, and exposed skin can increase contact with biting or stinging insects.

Mosquito bites, ant stings, and bee or wasp stings are examples. The response depends on the insect and the individual’s sensitivity.

Common symptoms

A small itchy bump, localized warmth, tenderness, or swelling may develop. Stings often cause immediate sharp pain.

Spreading hives, throat symptoms, breathing difficulty, vomiting with other allergic features, dizziness, or collapse requires urgent assessment.

Step-by-step first aid
1. Move away from the insect or nest.
2. Remove a retained stinger promptly; do not delay while searching for a preferred tool.
3. Wash the area gently.
4. Remove tight jewelry if swelling may develop.
5. Apply a cloth-wrapped cold pack for short periods.
6. Avoid scratching and monitor for worsening symptoms.
For an attached tick, use appropriate removal guidance: fine-tipped tweezers close to the skin and steady upward traction. Do not burn or coat it with chemicals. Red Cross

Treatment and medicines

An age-appropriate oral antihistamine may reduce itching. A short course of suitable hydrocortisone on intact skin may help a local reaction when advised. Paracetamol may relieve pain.

Antibiotics are not routine treatment for an uncomplicated bite or sting. Suspected anaphylaxis requires adrenaline and emergency care, not antihistamine alone. NHS

Prevention and return to sport

Use appropriate clothing and a locally recommended repellent according to its label. Keep food covered and avoid disturbing nests.

A mild local reaction may allow participation if comfortable. Mouth or throat stings, significant eye-area involvement, multiple stings, or a previous severe sting reaction warrants prompt medical advice. NHS
9. Abrasions or grazes

What the injury is

An abrasion is a superficial injury in which rubbing or scraping removes part of the skin surface. It commonly occurs after a fall onto a court, track, concrete, or rough ground.

Although shallow, it can be painful and contain dirt or grit.

Main causes and why it happens

Falls, sliding, collisions, and contact with rough equipment can scrape away the protective skin barrier. Contamination creates an opportunity for infection.

A wound that remains dirty or contains embedded material requires professional cleaning.

Common symptoms

Stinging pain, a raw appearance, mild bleeding, and clear wound fluid may occur. Increasing pain, spreading warmth or discoloration, pus, or fever suggests possible infection.

Step-by-step first aid
1. Stop activity and wash your hands; use gloves when available.
2. Control bleeding with direct pressure using clean gauze.
3. Rinse the wound thoroughly with clean running water.
4. Gently clean surrounding skin.
5. Do not dig out deeply embedded material.
6. Apply an appropriate non-adherent dressing.
7. Replace the dressing when wet, dirty, loose, or otherwise required.
For a clean minor wound, a thin layer of plain petroleum jelly can help maintain a moist healing surface beneath a suitable dressing. NHS

Treatment and medicines

Routine antibiotics are unnecessary for a clean, uninfected minor abrasion. Do not repeatedly pour alcohol or other harsh chemicals into the wound.

Paracetamol may be considered for pain when appropriate. A clinician should assess contamination and tetanus vaccination status.

Prevention and return to sport

Maintain safe surfaces, suitable footwear, and appropriate protective clothing. Keep wounds covered and avoid sharing towels.

Participation requires bleeding to have stopped, the wound to be clean and securely covered, and movement to be safe. A dressing is not permission to continue if it repeatedly opens or leaks. aad.org
10. Cuts or lacerations

What the injury is

A cut or laceration is a break in the skin caused by a sharp edge, impact, or tearing force. It may be superficial or extend into fat, muscle, tendons, nerves, or blood vessels.

A small-looking wound can still be deep, particularly on the hand or near a joint.

Main causes and symptoms

Broken equipment, sharp edges, falls, collisions, and contact with another person’s teeth can cause wounds. Symptoms include bleeding, pain, separated edges, and swelling.

Numbness, weakness, impaired movement, or an embedded object suggests a more significant injury. A wound caused by teeth needs assessment because contamination risk differs from a clean cut. University Hospitals Sussex NHS Foundation Trust

Step-by-step first aid
1. Stop participation and use gloves when available.
2. Apply firm, continuous direct pressure with clean gauze or cloth.
3. Activate emergency help immediately for heavy, spurting, or uncontrolled bleeding.
4. Do not repeatedly lift the dressing to check.
5. Do not remove a deeply embedded object; avoid pressing directly on it.
6. Once minor bleeding is controlled, clean and cover an appropriate superficial wound.
7. Arrange assessment for deep, gaping, contaminated, or function-affecting wounds. Red Cross
Treatment and medicines

A clinician decides whether stitches, medical adhesive, strips, debridement, or other treatment is needed. Do not close a dirty or deep wound with household glue.

Antibiotics depend on the wound and infection risk, not merely the presence of a cut.

Tetanus prevention depends on vaccination history and wound type. With a completed primary series, CDC guidance considers a booster at 10 or more years for clean minor wounds and 5 or more years for dirty or major wounds. Unknown or incomplete vaccination requires assessment; some patients also need tetanus immune globulin. Antibiotics do not replace tetanus prevention. NHS

Prevention and return to sport

Inspect equipment and surfaces and use appropriate protection. Return must account for closure strength, body location, contact risk, and clinician instructions. Do not compete with active bleeding or a wound likely to reopen.
11. Friction burns or court/turf burns

What the injury is

A friction burn occurs when forceful rubbing damages the skin. Sliding on a court or artificial turf often produces an abrasion, sometimes with an additional heat-related component.

It overlaps with grazes rather than always being a separate disease. Some injuries are deeper than their initial appearance suggests.

Main causes and why it happens

Sliding, diving, falling, or being dragged across a rough surface can remove skin. Longer contact, greater force, and exposed skin can increase damage.

Sand, grit, or artificial-turf material may contaminate the wound. Baylor College of Medicine Blog Network

Common symptoms

Burning or stinging pain, tenderness, a scraped appearance, superficial bleeding, and clear fluid may occur.

Extensive blistering, white or leathery tissue, reduced sensation, deep tissue loss, or substantial pain requires assessment. Less pain does not necessarily mean a deep injury is minor.

Step-by-step first aid
1. Stop activity.
2. Check for associated head, joint, or other injuries.
3. Control bleeding with clean direct pressure.
4. Rinse away loose contamination using clean running water.
5. Do not aggressively scrub or dig out embedded particles.
6. Cover with a suitable non-adherent dressing.
7. Obtain assessment if the wound is extensive, deep, persistently dirty, or near a functionally important area.
If a genuine thermal burn is also present, begin appropriate burn cooling with cool running water while arranging advice. Do not apply ice directly or pull away material stuck to burned skin. Baylor College of Medicine Blog Network

Treatment and medicines

Clean superficial wounds generally need protection and appropriate wound care rather than automatic antibiotics. Pain relief may be used when suitable.

Deeper burns may require specialized dressings, debridement, or referral. Do not apply toothpaste, butter, or unverified remedies. Assess tetanus protection where indicated.

Prevention and return to sport

Use sport-appropriate protective clothing, maintain safe surfaces, and practise safe movement techniques.

Avoid continued sliding on the injured area. Return only when bleeding has stopped, the wound can remain securely covered, and movement is comfortable and safe. Deep friction burns require clinical guidance. Baylor College of Medicine Blog Network


E. Skin infections that can affect athletes

1. Athlete’s foot — tinea pedis

What the condition is

Athlete’s foot is a fungal infection affecting the skin of the feet. Dermatophyte fungi cause it; it is not caused by worms.

It often affects the spaces between the toes but may involve the soles, sides of the feet, or occasionally produce blisters. Not every peeling or itchy foot rash is fungal—eczema and footwear-related dermatitis can resemble it.

Main causes and why it happens during sport

Fungi can spread through infected skin particles, shared personal items, and contaminated communal surfaces. Warm, moist footwear helps create conditions in which infection can persist.

Repeated training, damp socks, limited drying time between events, and barefoot walking in shared changing areas can contribute. dermnetnz.org

Common symptoms

Itching, burning, scaling, softened skin between the toes, cracks, and discomfort may occur. Extensive inflammation, pus, increasing pain, or spreading warmth suggests a complication or another diagnosis.

Cracked skin can provide an entry point for bacteria.

Initial care
1. Gently wash the feet.
2. Dry carefully, particularly between the toes.
3. Change damp socks.
4. Allow shoes to dry between uses.
5. Avoid scratching or sharing towels and footwear.
6. Arrange assessment if the diagnosis is uncertain or the condition is extensive.
Treatment and medicines

An appropriate topical antifungal—such as terbinafine, clotrimazole, or miconazole—may be recommended. Product schedules differ, so follow the specific instructions rather than using one duration for every preparation.

Do not apply a steroid cream alone to suspected ringworm. Persistent, extensive, or recurrent infection may require testing and prescription treatment. CDC

Prevention and return to sport

Wear footwear in communal showers, change socks regularly, and avoid shared shoes. Seek clinical advice if diabetes, poor circulation, or reduced foot sensation is present.

Participation depends on skin integrity, comfort, treatment, and event rules. Painful cracks, open lesions, or suspected bacterial infection need review before continued activity. CDC
2. Body ringworm — tinea corporis

What the condition is

Tinea corporis is a dermatophyte fungal infection of body skin. The name “ringworm” refers to its appearance, not a parasite.

A typical lesion is an enlarging scaly patch with a more active border. It does not always form a perfect ring or have obvious central clearing, particularly after inappropriate steroid treatment.

Main causes and why it happens

Transmission can occur through contact with an infected person or animal, contaminated items, or spread from another infected body site.

Close-contact sports create opportunities for transmission. Ringworm associated with wrestling is often called tinea gladiatorum. CDC

Common symptoms

Itching, scaling, an expanding border, and changes in skin color may occur. Similar-looking conditions include eczema, psoriasis, and other infections.

A clinician may examine skin scrapings when the diagnosis is uncertain or treatment fails.

Initial care
1. Report the lesion to the medical team.
2. Avoid direct skin contact involving the affected area.
3. Keep it clean and appropriately dry.
4. Do not share towels, clothing, or protective equipment.
5. Avoid shaving over the rash.
6. Arrange assessment before contact competition.
Treatment and medicines

Localized infection may respond to an appropriate topical antifungal. Extensive disease, follicular involvement, treatment failure, or involvement of the scalp may require oral medication and further assessment.

Scalp ringworm is different: ordinary skin creams alone are insufficient treatment.

Avoid steroid-only creams and unsupervised combination products. A rash that becomes less red while continuing to spread is not necessarily improving. CDC

Prevention and return to sport

Clean shared equipment, launder clothing, and assess potentially infected household contacts or pets through the appropriate professional.

Contact-sport return depends on the relevant federation’s treatment and lesion requirements. Do not assume that applying cream once or covering a lesion makes the athlete eligible. Obtain documented clearance where required. CDC
3. Jock itch — tinea cruris

What the condition is

Jock itch is a dermatophyte fungal infection involving the groin and adjacent upper thighs.

It is not the same as every groin rash. Chafing, candidal intertrigo, eczema, and bacterial conditions can produce similar symptoms.

Main causes and why it happens

Warmth, moisture, friction, and occlusive clothing encourage the infection. Fungi may spread from infected feet or nails, contaminated towels, or another person.

Long periods in sweaty underwear or uniforms can aggravate the problem. dermnetnz.org

Common symptoms

An itchy, scaly rash often begins in a groin fold and spreads onto the inner thigh. It may have a raised border and be more pronounced on one side.

Marked genital involvement, discharge, ulcers, significant pain, or failure to improve warrants assessment rather than assuming uncomplicated jock itch.

Initial care
1. Change out of sweaty clothing.
2. Wash gently and dry the folds carefully.
3. Use clean, breathable underwear.
4. Avoid sharing towels.
5. Assess for athlete’s foot or another possible source.
6. Seek advice when the appearance or diagnosis is uncertain.
Treatment and medicines

A suitable topical antifungal may be recommended. Use it for the instructed course.

Do not use strong steroid creams in the groin without clinical direction. Steroids can disguise fungal infection and cause skin thinning in this sensitive area.

Persistent cases may require fungal testing, a different diagnosis, or prescription treatment. dermnetnz.org

Prevention and return to sport

Keep clothing clean, change damp garments promptly, and treat confirmed infection at other body sites.

A noncontact activity may remain possible if comfortable and hygienically manageable. Contact sport requires consideration of exposed lesions, transmission risk, and event rules. Continued painful rubbing can also delay recovery.
4. Folliculitis

What the condition is

Folliculitis is inflammation around hair follicles. It can be infectious or noninfectious.

Bacteria, yeast, friction, shaving, and skin occlusion can contribute. Therefore, every follicular bump does not require an antibiotic.

Main causes and why it happens

Sweaty tight clothing, protective equipment, shaving, and repeated rubbing may damage or obstruct follicles. Contaminated recreational water can cause certain bacterial forms.

The cause matters: bacterial folliculitis, yeast-associated folliculitis, and shaving irritation need different management. dermnetnz.org

Common symptoms

Small bumps or pustules centered on hairs, itching, tenderness, and irritation are typical.

Deepening pain, a larger lump, spreading inflammation, or fever suggests progression or another diagnosis.

Initial care
1. Reduce friction and stop shaving over the area.
2. Change damp clothing.
3. Wash gently.
4. Avoid squeezing or scratching bumps.
5. Use a clean, comfortably warm compress for localized discomfort when appropriate.
6. Seek assessment for widespread, recurrent, painful, or worsening lesions.
Treatment and medicines

Mild cases may improve after removing the trigger. A clinician may prescribe topical or oral treatment for a confirmed infectious cause.

Antibiotics do not treat yeast folliculitis. Conversely, antifungal creams do not treat ordinary bacterial infections.

Avoid applying multiple creams or using leftover antibiotics. A swab or other assessment may be useful in persistent or recurrent cases. dermnetnz.org

Prevention and return to sport

Keep equipment and clothing clean, avoid shared razors, and review shaving practices. Proper pool maintenance is important for water-associated infection.

Noninfectious shaving irritation does not carry the same transmission risk as bacterial folliculitis. Until the cause is established, draining or suspicious lesions require medical review before close-contact sport. CDC
5. Boils — furuncles

What the condition is

A boil is a deeper infection involving a hair follicle and surrounding tissue. It forms a painful lump that may develop a pus-filled center.

Boils are commonly associated with Staphylococcus aureus. Some infections involve MRSA, but appearance alone cannot determine antibiotic resistance.

Main causes and why it happens

Bacteria can enter through damaged skin or follicles. Friction, minor abrasions, close contact, and shared contaminated items can contribute.

A boil is not proof of poor hygiene, and a “spider bite” appearance does not exclude bacterial infection. NHS

Common symptoms

A tender, warm lump may enlarge, become increasingly painful, and drain pus. Several connected boils form a carbuncle.

Facial lesions, spreading inflammation, fever, or significant illness require prompt assessment.

First aid and initial care
1. Stop activities that rub the lesion.
2. Do not squeeze, lance, or pierce it.
3. Apply a clean, comfortably warm compress.
4. If it drains, cover it with a clean dressing.
5. Wash hands before and after dressing care.
6. Arrange medical assessment, especially in an athlete attending a shared event.
Treatment and medicines

A clinician may need to drain the boil. Antibiotics may also be required depending on its size, surrounding infection, systemic symptoms, and patient factors.

Pain relief may be considered when appropriate. Antibiotic selection should account for local resistance and testing where indicated; there is no universal antibiotic for every boil. NHS

Prevention and return to sport

Do not share towels, razors, or clothing. Recurrent boils need review; decolonization measures should only be used under an appropriate clinical plan.

Do not participate in contact sport with an active draining boil. Swimming pools and shared water facilities should be avoided until medically appropriate. Covering pus does not remove transmission risk. NHS
6. Skin abscesses

What the condition is

A skin abscess is a localized collection of pus within or beneath the skin. A boil is one type of abscess, so these categories overlap.

An abscess can also arise independently of a hair follicle.

Main causes and why it happens

Bacteria may enter through a wound, follicle, or other skin break. The immune response produces a collection containing inflammatory cells, organisms, and damaged tissue.

Repeated friction and untreated skin injuries may contribute in athletes. NHS

Common symptoms

A painful lump, warmth, swelling, tenderness, and sometimes a softer center may occur. Fever or spreading inflammation can accompany it.

Not every abscess has a visible opening. Do not wait for it to burst before seeking help.

First aid
1. Stop contact activity.
2. Do not puncture or squeeze the swelling.
3. Cover any drainage with a clean dressing.
4. Avoid sharing personal items.
5. Obtain clinical assessment.
6. Seek urgent care for rapid progression, severe pain, fever, or significant illness.
Treatment and medicines

A drainable abscess often requires incision and drainage by a qualified clinician. Antibiotics alone may not adequately treat a significant pus collection.

Antibiotics are added when indicated, taking account of systemic illness, surrounding infection, immune status, and organism risk. A pus sample may help guide treatment.

After drainage, follow wound-care and follow-up instructions. Do not remove packing or manipulate the wound unless instructed. NHS

Prevention and return to sport

Protect skin injuries, report suspicious lumps early, and clean shared equipment.

Return requires satisfactory treatment, controlled drainage, safe wound management, and compliance with contact-sport rules. A newly drained abscess does not automatically become safe to cover and compete with.

Repeated abscesses warrant assessment for contributing conditions and an individualized prevention plan. CDC
7. Impetigo

What the condition is

Impetigo is a contagious superficial bacterial skin infection. Staphylococcus aureus, group A streptococci, or both may be involved.

It can develop where skin has been scratched, grazed, or affected by eczema, although a visible preceding injury is not always present.

Main causes and symptoms

Transmission can occur through contact with lesions or contaminated personal items.

Typical lesions include shallow sores with yellow or honey-colored crusting. Another form produces fragile blisters. These appearances are suggestive but can overlap with other skin diseases. CDC

Initial care
1. Report the suspected infection.
2. Wash hands before and after touching the area.
3. Clean gently without picking or aggressively removing crusts.
4. Cover lesions where practical.
5. Keep towels, clothing, and bedding separate.
6. Avoid contact competition pending assessment.
Treatment and medicines

Treatment depends on the extent and type of infection. Localized cases may receive an appropriate topical treatment; more extensive, blistering, or systemically symptomatic illness may require oral antibiotics.

Mupirocin is one example of a topical antibiotic used in selected cases, but local resistance and prescribing guidance matter.

Do not routinely combine topical and oral antibiotics or repeatedly reuse an old prescription. Follow the prescribed course and seek review if lesions spread or fail to improve. idsociety.org

Warning signs

Fever, rapidly spreading pain or swelling, extensive blistering, or a generally unwell child requires prompt medical attention.

Prevention and return to sport

Treat underlying eczema appropriately, keep nails short, and avoid shared personal items.

School attendance guidance is not the same as clearance for wrestling or another close-contact event. Return depends on treatment, lesion condition, absence of new lesions or drainage where required, and the governing rules. Do not use a generic school exclusion interval as automatic sports clearance. CDC
8. Cellulitis

What the condition is

Cellulitis is a bacterial infection involving deeper skin layers and underlying tissue. It is different from a small superficial rash and normally requires antibiotic treatment.

An abscess may coexist, but cellulitis does not always contain a collection that can be drained.

Main causes and why it happens

Bacteria can enter through cuts, grazes, cracks, insect-related skin damage, or other skin breaks. Athlete’s foot can create cracks between the toes that provide an entry point.

The entry site may be too small to notice. nhs.uk

Common symptoms

Pain, warmth, swelling, and spreading redness or discoloration are typical. Fever and feeling unwell may occur.

On darker skin, color changes can be subtle, making warmth, tenderness, and swelling particularly important.

First aid and immediate action
1. Stop strenuous activity.
2. Obtain prompt medical assessment.
3. Rest the affected part and elevate it when practical.
4. Protect any associated wound.
5. Do not massage, squeeze, or attempt drainage.
6. Seek emergency care for rapid progression, severe pain, confusion, faintness, or significant systemic illness.
Infection around an eye requires urgent assessment.

Treatment and medicines

A clinician selects an antibiotic according to severity, likely organisms, allergies, and local guidance. Severe illness may need hospital treatment and intravenous antibiotics.

An antifungal or steroid cream alone will not treat cellulitis. A coexisting abscess may need drainage.

Seek reassessment if symptoms worsen or do not begin improving within the timeframe specified by the clinician—commonly within two to three days. NICE

Prevention and return to sport

Treat skin breaks and confirmed fungal infection, and maintain appropriate skin care.

Cellulitis itself is not usually spread by ordinary casual contact, but bacteria from an associated draining lesion can spread. Return depends on clinical recovery, wound status, and medical advice. Fever, pain, or continuing spread means the athlete is not ready for strenuous participation. nhs.uk
9. Herpes simplex skin infection, including herpes gladiatorum

What the condition is

Herpes simplex virus causes recurrent skin infections. Herpes gladiatorum, most commonly associated with HSV-1, refers to infection occurring in athletes through close contact.

It often affects the face, neck, arms, or trunk in contact sports. It is not necessarily sexually acquired and should not be stigmatized.

Main causes and why it happens

Direct skin contact is important in transmission. Abrasions and close physical contact can facilitate spread.

After infection, the virus remains in the body and may reactivate. Clearing an outbreak does not eliminate the virus permanently. MN Dept. of Health

Common symptoms

Burning, tingling, tenderness, or itching may precede grouped blisters. Blisters can break, form shallow sores, and crust.

A first episode may include fever or enlarged glands. The appearance can be mistaken for impetigo or folliculitis.

Initial care
1. Stop contact participation immediately.
2. Avoid touching or picking lesions.
3. Wash hands after contact with the area.
4. Avoid sharing towels, razors, or other personal items.
5. Arrange prompt clinical assessment.
6. Obtain urgent care for eye-area lesions, eye pain, light sensitivity, or altered vision.
A fresh lesion swab may help confirm the diagnosis. AAOS

Treatment and medicines

Prescription oral antivirals, such as acyclovir or valacyclovir, may shorten outbreaks and reduce viral activity. Treatment is generally more useful when started early.

Recurrent outbreaks may lead a clinician to consider suppressive treatment. Antibiotics do not treat herpes unless a separate bacterial infection is present.

Prevention and return to sport

Report early tingling or new lesions and follow an individualized management plan.

Active herpes lesions cannot simply be covered to permit close-contact competition. Return requires the applicable antiviral-treatment interval, lesion-healing requirements, symptom recovery, and medical clearance under the sport’s rules. my.clevelandclinic.org
10. Plantar warts

What the condition is

Plantar warts are growths on the soles caused by certain human papillomavirus types. They are different from corns and calluses, although they may resemble them.

Pressure can push the wart inward beneath thickened skin, making walking or running painful.

Main causes and why it happens

The virus enters through small skin breaks. Contact with infected skin or contaminated surfaces may contribute.

Shared changing facilities and repeated foot trauma are relevant exposures, but not every athlete using a communal shower develops warts.

Common symptoms

A rough or thickened area, interrupted skin lines, small dark dots, and pain with pressure may occur.

An uncertain, changing, bleeding, or unusually painful lesion needs examination rather than automatic wart treatment.

Initial care
1. Do not cut or pick the lesion.
2. Keep the foot clean.
3. Use appropriate footwear.
4. Avoid sharing files, pumice stones, socks, or towels.
5. Consider pressure-relieving padding if appropriate.
6. Obtain assessment if pain changes walking or running mechanics.
Treatment and medicines

Some warts resolve without treatment, but this can take months or longer.

Salicylic acid is a common treatment for selected plantar warts. It can injure surrounding skin, so use the correct product and follow instructions. People with diabetes, poor circulation, reduced sensation, or an uncertain diagnosis should not self-treat with destructive preparations.

A clinician may use cryotherapy or other treatments. Several sessions may be needed; no method guarantees immediate clearance. aad.org

Prevention and return to sport

Avoid picking or shaving lesions, protect feet in shared areas, and keep personal foot-care equipment separate.

Participation depends on pain, treatment effects, skin integrity, and event requirements. Treatment-created blisters or open areas may temporarily prevent safe running or swimming. Follow individualized advice rather than trying to remove the wart immediately before competition. aad.org
11. Molluscum contagiosum

What the condition is

Molluscum contagiosum is a superficial viral infection caused by a poxvirus. It produces small, smooth bumps, often with a central indentation.

It is common in children and can also affect adolescents and adults. It does not imply poor hygiene.

Main causes and why it happens

Transmission occurs through skin contact, contaminated items, or spread from one body area to another through scratching or shaving.

Shared towels and close-contact activities are relevant. Eczema can make surrounding skin more irritated and scratching more likely.

Common symptoms

Bumps may be skin-colored, pink, or pale. They are often painless but can itch or develop surrounding dermatitis.

Redness alone does not always mean bacterial infection; lesions can become inflamed while resolving. Increasing pain, pus, spreading inflammation, or fever warrants assessment. CDC

Initial care
1. Do not squeeze, scrape, or puncture bumps.
2. Avoid shaving over them.
3. Keep hands and nails clean.
4. Cover lesions when recommended to reduce contact.
5. Do not share towels or equipment touching affected skin.
6. Seek advice for uncertain, extensive, genital, eye-area, or repeatedly irritated lesions.
Treatment and medicines

Healthy people often recover without treatment, although clearance can take many months.

A clinician may recommend treatment based on symptoms, site, spread, eczema, immune status, and sport participation. Options can include carefully selected topical treatment or office procedures.

Do not apply ordinary wart acids or online “molluscum cures” without advice. Some cause burns or scarring. Imiquimod is not recommended by CDC for children with molluscum. CDC

Prevention and return to sport

School exclusion is generally unnecessary, but sport-specific requirements still apply.

Some activities may permit secure covering of lesions; close-contact governing bodies may impose stricter clearance or treatment requirements. For swimming, follow guidance on watertight coverage and avoid participation with open sores. Do not share towels, goggles, or other personal items. CDC


F. Sudden muscle, ligament, and joint injuries

1. Exercise-associated muscle cramps

What the condition is

An exercise-associated muscle cramp is a sudden, painful, involuntary contraction during or after activity. The muscle may feel hard or visibly tighten.

A cramp differs from a muscle tear, although both can cause abrupt pain.

Main causes and contributing factors

There is no single explanation for every episode. Fatigue and changes in neuromuscular control are important contributors. Fluid and electrolyte losses may contribute in some circumstances, but a cramp does not automatically prove dehydration, low potassium, or heat illness.

Symptoms

Typical features are sudden tightening, pain, and temporary difficulty moving the muscle. Persistent localized pain, substantial bruising, or weakness after the tightening settles suggests another injury.

First aid
1. Stop activity.
2. Position the athlete comfortably.
3. Gently stretch the affected muscle and hold without bouncing.
4. Release if stretching produces sharp or increasing pain.
5. Allow rest and appropriate drinking if fully alert.
6. Assess for heat illness or other symptoms.
Treatment and medicines

Gentle static stretching is a supported treatment for an active cramp. Routine medication is unnecessary. Quinine and tonic water should not be used as cramp treatments.

Do not force excessive water or salt intake.

Prevention and return to sport

Review workload, conditioning, previous episodes, nutrition, and hydration individually. Stretching before activity does not reliably prevent all cramps.

Return only after the cramp resolves, normal movement and strength return, and no concerning cause is identified. Recurrent or widespread cramps need assessment. pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov
2. Muscle bruises or contusions

What the injury is

A muscle contusion occurs when a direct blow crushes muscle fibers and small blood vessels without necessarily breaking the skin. Bleeding within the tissue causes swelling and discoloration.

Main causes

Examples include a knee striking the thigh, collision with another player, or landing against a hard surface.

A deep contusion can be significant even when the visible bruise initially looks small.

Symptoms

Pain, tenderness, swelling, stiffness, and reduced movement are typical. Bruising may become more visible over the following days.

First aid
1. Stop activity.
2. Protect the area from further impact.
3. Apply a wrapped cold pack for comfort.
4. Use appropriate gentle compression if tolerated.
5. Avoid deep massage and forceful stretching.
6. Obtain assessment for severe pain, substantial swelling, or impaired function.
Increasing pain with a tense swollen muscle, numbness, or weakness may indicate compartment syndrome, which is an emergency.

Treatment and medicines

Treatment ranges from protection and rehabilitation to specialist care for significant bleeding or complications. A suitable painkiller may help, but medicine should not be used to permit repeated blows to the injured area.

Aggressive early treatment can worsen tissue damage.

Prevention and return to sport

Use appropriate protective equipment and safe technique. Return requires comfortable movement, adequate strength, and the ability to perform sport-specific tasks without compensating.

Significant thigh contusions need individualized management. A coach should not force the knee into a prescribed position without clinical assessment. AAOS
3. Ankle sprain

What the injury is

An ankle sprain is stretching or tearing of the ligaments stabilizing the ankle. Lateral sprains, affecting the outer ligaments, are common. Other patterns include inner-ligament and syndesmotic—“high ankle”—injuries.

Main causes

Landing awkwardly, stepping on another player’s foot, turning suddenly, or moving over an uneven surface can injure the ligaments.

Symptoms

Pain, swelling, bruising, and difficulty bearing weight may occur. Symptoms alone cannot reliably distinguish a sprain from a fracture or growth plate injury.

First aid
1. Stop running or jumping.
2. Protect the ankle and avoid forced weight-bearing.
3. Use a wrapped cold pack for comfort.
4. Apply suitable support or compression if trained.
5. Elevate when practical.
6. Arrange assessment for significant pain, bony tenderness, or difficulty walking.
Treatment and medicines

Treatment may include a brace, crutches, temporary immobilization, and progressive rehabilitation. Severe or unusual injuries require further assessment.

Pain relief may be appropriate, but a medicine cannot restore ankle stability.

Prevention

Balance training, strength work, landing practice, and—in selected athletes—bracing or taping can reduce recurrence risk. Previous ankle injury deserves attention before competition.

Return to sport

The athlete should demonstrate suitable movement, strength, balance, confidence, and sport-specific function. Walking without pain alone is insufficient preparation for cutting, jumping, or landing.

Persistent pain above the ankle, repeated giving way, or failure to improve requires review. AAOS
4. Knee ligament injury, including ACL or MCL injury

What the injury is

Knee ligaments provide stability. The ACL helps control forward movement and rotation of the tibia; the MCL supports the inner knee.

Injuries range from partial damage to complete tears and may coexist with meniscus, cartilage, or bone injury.

Main causes

ACL injuries can occur during pivoting, sudden stopping, or awkward landing without contact. MCL injury commonly follows force that stresses the inner knee, including a blow to its outer side.

Symptoms

A pop, swelling, pain, instability, or difficulty continuing may occur. Not every ACL injury causes an audible pop.

First aid
1. Remove the athlete from play.
2. Avoid testing the knee repeatedly.
3. Support it comfortably.
4. Limit weight-bearing if painful or unstable.
5. Arrange prompt assessment.
A severely unstable or deformed knee needs emergency evaluation, even if it appears to move back into place.

Treatment and medicines

Assessment may include examination and imaging. Many isolated MCL injuries are managed with bracing and rehabilitation. ACL management is individualized; some athletes require reconstruction, particularly for instability and return to pivoting sport.

Surgery is not decided from the word “tear” alone. Associated injuries, skeletal maturity, and goals matter. AAOS

Prevention and return to sport

Neuromuscular training should address landing, balance, strength, and direction changes.

Return requires structured rehabilitation and objective assessment—not merely a fixed number of weeks or reduced pain. Young athletes need specialist decisions that account for growth and reinjury risk. pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov
5. Wrist sprain

What the injury is

A wrist sprain damages the ligaments connecting wrist bones. It may be mild or involve a significant tear.

Main causes

Falling onto an outstretched hand, forceful twisting, or impact during a game can cause injury.

Symptoms

Pain, swelling, bruising, tenderness, and difficulty gripping or loading the hand may occur.

A small fracture—particularly a scaphoid fracture—can be mistaken for a sprain. Limited swelling does not exclude a significant injury.

First aid
1. Stop gripping, throwing, or weight-bearing through the hand.
2. Remove rings or tight wrist items if this can be done easily.
3. Support the wrist in a comfortable position.
4. Apply a wrapped cold pack.
5. Arrange assessment for substantial or persistent symptoms.
Treatment and medicines

A clinician may recommend a splint and appropriate rehabilitation. Significant ligament injury may need specialist treatment.

A normal initial X-ray does not resolve every persistent wrist complaint. Continued thumb-side wrist pain after a fall needs reassessment.

Pain medicine should not be used to allow painful push-ups, catching, or lifting.

Prevention and return to sport

Use sport-appropriate protection and develop safe falling technique where relevant.

Return requires suitable motion, grip, stability, and tolerance of the sport’s loading demands. A brace is not a substitute for diagnosis or clearance. AAOS
6. Finger sprain or jammed finger

What the injury is

A jammed finger may involve a ligament sprain, volar plate injury, tendon damage, fracture, or dislocation. “Jammed” describes the mechanism rather than a complete diagnosis.

Main causes

A ball striking the fingertip or a finger being forced backward or sideways can injure supporting structures.

Symptoms

Pain, swelling, bruising, and stiffness are common. Inability to straighten or bend a joint normally can indicate tendon injury.

First aid
1. Stop play.
2. Remove rings promptly if easily possible.
3. Support the finger without forcing alignment.
4. Apply a wrapped cold pack.
5. Seek assessment for deformity, marked swelling, bony tenderness, or loss of movement.
Do not pull on the finger to “unjam” it.

Treatment and medicines

Treatment depends on the structure injured. A clinician may select a specific splint or buddy taping for an appropriate stable injury.

Buddy taping is not suitable for every finger injury. Some tendon injuries require a particular splint position and uninterrupted treatment.

Pain relief may improve comfort but does not exclude fracture.

Prevention and return to sport

Use safe catching technique and appropriate protection.

Return depends on diagnosis, stability, function, and the ability to protect the finger safely. Persistent swelling, deformity, or motion loss should not be ignored because finger injuries can lead to lasting stiffness or dysfunction. AAOS
7. Thumb ligament sprain

What the injury is

A thumb sprain damages supporting ligaments, commonly the ulnar collateral ligament at the thumb’s main knuckle.

A complete tear can make pinch and grip unstable.

Main causes

The thumb may be forced away from the hand during a fall, catching a ball, or contact with another player.

Symptoms

Pain, swelling, bruising near the thumb base, weak pinch, and a feeling of looseness may occur. Some injuries pull off a small bone fragment.

First aid
1. Stop gripping and ball handling.
2. Remove rings if easily possible.
3. Support the thumb and wrist.
4. Apply a wrapped cold pack.
5. Arrange prompt assessment.
Do not repeatedly push the thumb sideways to test stability.

Treatment and medicines

A partial injury may need a thumb-supporting splint or cast. A complete tear or displaced ligament injury may require surgery.

Early diagnosis matters because some significant tears do not heal reliably with casual taping.

Pain medicine does not restore ligament stability.

Prevention and return to sport

Use appropriate technique and protective equipment where relevant. After injury, complete prescribed rehabilitation.

Return requires adequate healing, stable pinch and grip, appropriate movement, and sport-specific function. The athlete should not return merely because tape makes catching temporarily tolerable. AAOS
8. Hamstring strain

What the injury is

A hamstring strain damages muscles or tendons at the back of the thigh. Severity ranges from limited fiber injury to a major tear or tendon avulsion.

Main causes

Sprinting, sudden acceleration, kicking, and forceful lengthening can overload the hamstrings.

Previous injury and inadequate recovery can contribute, but no single factor explains every strain.

Symptoms

Sudden posterior-thigh pain, weakness, altered running, and later bruising may occur. A pop, substantial weakness, or pain close to the buttock can indicate a more serious injury.

First aid
1. Stop sprinting immediately.
2. Protect the leg and avoid painful walking.
3. Apply a wrapped cold pack for comfort.
4. Avoid forceful stretching and deep massage.
5. Arrange assessment if function is substantially impaired.
Treatment and medicines

Rehabilitation progressively restores movement, strength, and running capacity. Severe tears or tendon avulsions may require surgery.

In a growing athlete, a bony avulsion can resemble a muscle strain and needs assessment. AAOS

Prevention and return to sport

Progressive sprint exposure, strength training, and adequate recovery are important.

Return requires more than painless walking or jogging. The athlete needs appropriate strength and tolerance of acceleration, high-speed running, and other sport demands.

Returning too early increases recurrence risk. Avoid promising one recovery time for every hamstring strain. Sports Medicine Australia
9. Quadriceps strain

What the injury is

A quadriceps strain damages muscle or tendon at the front of the thigh. It differs from a contusion caused by a direct blow.

Main causes

Sprinting, kicking, jumping, or forceful lengthening can overload the muscle. A sudden injury may occur while the muscle is producing force.

Symptoms

Front-thigh pain, tenderness, weakness, swelling, and bruising may develop. Running, kicking, or straightening the knee can be painful.

A visible gap or inability to actively straighten the knee needs urgent assessment for a major muscle or tendon injury.

First aid
1. Stop the activity.
2. Protect the leg.
3. Avoid forced knee bending or stretching.
4. Use a wrapped cold pack for comfort.
5. Obtain assessment for substantial weakness, swelling, or difficulty walking.
Treatment and medicines

Treatment commonly involves activity modification followed by graded rehabilitation. Severe injury may require imaging or specialist care.

Pain relief may be appropriate, but analgesia does not establish readiness to kick or sprint.

Prevention and return to sport

Progressive strength work, sensible workload increases, and appropriate preparation reduce relevant risks.

Return should include comfortable knee movement, restored strength, and successful progression through running, kicking, jumping, and landing as applicable.

A student with continuing weakness should not return simply because bruising has faded. AAOS
10. Calf strain

What the injury is

A calf strain damages one or more muscles at the back of the lower leg. It can range from a small injury to a substantial tear.

Main causes

Sudden acceleration, jumping, pushing off, or excessive muscle lengthening can cause injury.

Symptoms

A sharp calf pain, tenderness, weakness, swelling, and difficulty pushing off may occur.

A sensation of being kicked, a pop, or inability to push off normally may indicate Achilles tendon rupture, not merely a calf strain.

First aid
1. Stop running or jumping.
2. Protect the leg and avoid painful loading.
3. Apply a wrapped cold pack for comfort.
4. Avoid aggressive stretching or massage.
5. Arrange prompt assessment if a tendon rupture is possible.
Do not ask the athlete to perform repeated heel raises to prove the injury is minor.

Treatment and medicines

A confirmed strain is managed with an appropriate progression of movement, strength, and walking or running.

A ruptured Achilles needs a different protection and treatment pathway. An athlete may still manage some walking after rupture, so walking ability does not exclude it. fhft.nhs.uk

Prevention and return to sport

Restore calf endurance, power, and tolerance of acceleration and jumping.

New unexplained calf swelling, particularly with chest pain or breathlessness, needs urgent medical assessment rather than being assumed to be a training injury.

Return should follow functional recovery and diagnosis-specific advice. University Hospitals Plymouth NHS Trust
11. Groin or adductor strain

What the injury is

An adductor strain affects muscles or tendons on the inner thigh that help bring the leg toward the body’s midline and stabilize the hip.

Main causes

Kicking, sliding, sudden direction changes, or forceful stretching can overload these tissues.

Symptoms

Inner-thigh or groin pain, tenderness, weakness, and difficulty running, cutting, or moving the leg inward may occur.

Not every groin pain is an adductor strain. Hip disorders, hernias, bone injuries, and other conditions may cause similar symptoms.

First aid
1. Stop the painful activity.
2. Avoid wide stretching, forceful kicking, and repeated testing.
3. Allow comfortable supported rest.
4. Use a wrapped cold pack if helpful.
5. Seek assessment for substantial pain, difficulty walking, or uncertain diagnosis.
Sudden testicular pain or swelling is an emergency and should not be treated as a groin muscle injury.

Treatment and medicines

Treatment usually includes graded strengthening and progressive sport-specific loading. Severe or persistent symptoms may require further investigation.

Painkillers do not establish that the tissue can safely tolerate sprinting or kicking.

Prevention and return to sport

Build adductor strength, progress training gradually, and address previous symptoms.

Return should include pain-free or clinically acceptable strength testing and tolerance of sprinting, cutting, and kicking. Recurrent groin symptoms require a broader assessment rather than repeated rest alone. healthnz.govt.nz
12. Hip flexor strain

What the injury is

A hip flexor strain affects muscles or tendons that lift the thigh toward the trunk.

Main causes

Sprinting, kicking, jumping, or forceful movement into hip extension can overload the hip flexors.

Symptoms

Pain at the front of the hip or upper thigh, tenderness, weakness, and discomfort lifting the knee may occur.

In adolescents, a sudden forceful contraction can pull a fragment of bone from an attachment site. This avulsion injury can resemble a strain.

First aid
1. Stop the activity.
2. Avoid repeated high-knee movements or kicking.
3. Protect the leg if walking is painful.
4. Use a wrapped cold pack if comfortable.
5. Obtain assessment for a pop, substantial weakness, focal bony tenderness, or inability to bear weight.
Treatment and medicines

A confirmed strain often responds to activity modification and rehabilitation. Imaging may be needed when a fracture or avulsion is suspected.

Medicine can reduce symptoms but cannot distinguish a muscle injury from a bone injury.

Prevention and return to sport

Use progressive strength and workload preparation, and avoid sudden increases in kicking or sprinting volume.

Return requires restored hip movement, strength, and tolerance of relevant movements. Persistent deep hip or groin pain should be reassessed, particularly in a growing athlete. AAOS
13. Lower-back muscle strain

What the injury is

A lower-back strain affects muscles or tendons supporting the lumbar region. However, “back strain” should not be used automatically for every young athlete with back pain.

Main causes

Lifting, twisting, sudden force, or an unfamiliar workload may contribute. Other causes include bone stress injury, disc-related problems, and trauma.

Symptoms

Localized pain, stiffness, and muscle spasm may occur. Certain movements may be uncomfortable.

First aid
1. Stop the provoking activity.
2. Assess the mechanism and associated symptoms.
3. After serious injury is excluded, use a comfortable position and gentle activity as tolerated.
4. Avoid forceful manipulation or aggressive stretching.
5. Obtain assessment for persistent or significant pain.
After substantial trauma or suspected spinal injury, avoid unnecessary movement and obtain emergency assistance.

Emergency warning signs

New bladder or bowel dysfunction, numbness around the genitals or anus, or substantial weakness or numbness in both legs requires emergency assessment.

Treatment and medicines

For uncomplicated back pain, prolonged bed rest is generally discouraged. Rehabilitation and suitable activity modification are important.

Medication decisions are individualized. Adult back-pain recommendations should not automatically be applied to children. NHS

Prevention and return to sport

Progress lifting and training loads, maintain appropriate strength, and review technique.

Return requires tolerating the sport’s movements without significant pain or neurological symptoms. Persistent adolescent back pain—especially with repeated extension, night pain, or reduced function—needs clinical review rather than repeated labeling as a pulled muscle. CUH
14. Shoulder muscle or rotator cuff strain

What the injury is

The rotator cuff consists of muscles and tendons that help move and stabilize the shoulder. Injury may range from a strain to a significant tendon tear.

Main causes

Throwing, overhead hitting, lifting, falls, and sudden pulling forces can cause pain or injury.

In younger athletes, pain may also involve instability, a fracture, or a growth-related injury rather than a typical adult degenerative rotator cuff tear.

Symptoms

Shoulder pain, weakness, painful lifting, and reduced movement may occur.

An inability to lift the arm after trauma, obvious deformity, or numbness requires prompt assessment.

First aid
1. Stop throwing, hitting, or lifting.
2. Support the arm comfortably.
3. Use a wrapped cold pack for pain.
4. Do not force overhead movement.
5. Arrange assessment for marked weakness or traumatic onset.
Treatment and medicines

Treatment depends on the diagnosis. Rehabilitation may address movement, rotator cuff strength, shoulder-blade control, and sport-specific loading.

A significant traumatic tear may need specialist care. Prolonged sling use without direction can contribute to stiffness, while premature loading can aggravate injury.

Pain medicines or injections should not be used simply to enable same-day competition.

Prevention and return to sport

Progress overhead workloads, manage recovery, and address technique and conditioning.

Return requires suitable movement, strength, stability, and completion of a graded throwing or overhead progression. “It only hurts when serving” means the shoulder has not yet demonstrated tolerance of that task. AAOS
15. Knee meniscus injury

What the injury is

The menisci are fibrocartilage structures that distribute load and support knee function. A tear may occur alone or with ligament injury.

Main causes

Twisting or pivoting on a loaded knee can cause an acute tear. Contact is not always involved.

Symptoms

Joint-line pain, swelling, stiffness, catching, locking, or giving way may occur. An athlete can sometimes continue walking despite a tear.

First aid
1. Stop pivoting or running.
2. Support the knee comfortably.
3. Limit painful loading.
4. Apply a wrapped cold pack if helpful.
5. Seek assessment.
A knee that cannot straighten because it is mechanically locked needs prompt assessment. Do not force it straight.

Treatment and medicines

Treatment depends on tear pattern, location, symptoms, age, and associated injury. Options include rehabilitation or surgery.

When surgery is needed, preserving suitable meniscal tissue is particularly important in young athletes. Not every tear needs immediate surgery, and not every tear can heal with rest alone.

Pain relief does not remove a displaced fragment or repair the tear.

Prevention and return to sport

Maintain strength, movement control, and appropriate preparation for pivoting.

Return differs substantially between nonsurgical care, meniscal repair, and partial removal. Follow the actual treatment pathway rather than a general “knee injury” timeline. orthoinfo.org
16. Joint dislocation or partial dislocation

What the injury is

A dislocation is complete loss of normal joint alignment; a subluxation is partial loss. Both can damage ligaments, cartilage, nerves, and blood vessels.

Main causes

Falls, collisions, twisting, and forceful movement can affect the shoulder, finger, elbow, kneecap, or other joints.

Symptoms

Pain, deformity, restricted movement, swelling, and altered sensation may occur. A joint may move back spontaneously, leaving significant injury.

First aid
1. Stop activity.
2. Support the joint in the position found.
3. Do not pull, twist, or attempt reduction.
4. Remove tight items only if easily possible.
5. Arrange urgent medical care.
6. Activate emergency assistance for impaired circulation, numbness, major deformity, or unsafe transport.
Treatment and medicines

Qualified clinicians perform reduction with appropriate assessment, analgesia, and imaging when indicated. Rehabilitation or surgery may be necessary.

A tibiofemoral knee dislocation is particularly dangerous because major blood vessels can be injured—even after the joint appears aligned again. It is different from an isolated kneecap dislocation. nhs.uk

Prevention and return to sport

Address instability and complete rehabilitation. Repeated shoulder or kneecap episodes need assessment.

Return is not appropriate merely because the joint has “gone back in.” Bone, ligament, nerve, and vascular injury must be considered, followed by diagnosis-specific clearance. nhs.uk
17. Acute fracture

What the injury is

An acute fracture is a break caused by a single traumatic event. It may be displaced or nondisplaced and open or closed.

Main causes

Falls, collisions, direct blows, or twisting can fracture the hand, wrist, collarbone, ankle, foot, or other bones.

Symptoms

Pain, tenderness, swelling, bruising, and reduced function are common. Deformity is not always present, and some people can still move or bear weight.

First aid
1. Stop activity.
2. Support the injured part without straightening it.
3. Obtain medical assistance.
4. Cover an open wound with a clean dressing.
5. Control significant bleeding without pressing on exposed bone.
6. Do not push bone back inside.
7. Use a wrapped cold pack around a closed injury if appropriate.
8. Avoid unnecessary movement during transport.
Splinting should be performed by someone trained, without delaying emergency care.

Treatment and medicines

Treatment may involve a splint, cast, reduction, or surgery. Open fractures require urgent hospital care.

Pain relief is supportive; it does not replace alignment, stabilization, wound treatment, or follow-up.

Prevention and return to sport

Use appropriate protection and safe facilities. Maintain adequate nutrition and training preparation.

Return requires evidence of healing and adequate function under the treating clinician’s guidance. A cast or brace does not automatically permit contact sport, and reduced pain does not prove union. Red Cross
18. Growth plate injury

What the injury is

Growth plates are developing areas near the ends of growing bones. They are vulnerable to injury in children and adolescents.

Damage can affect future bone growth, making early recognition important.

Main causes

Falls, twisting, direct impact, and repetitive loading can injure growth plates. A mechanism that causes a ligament sprain in an adult may injure a developing bone in a child.

Symptoms

Pain near a joint or bone end, tenderness, swelling, reduced use, or difficulty bearing weight may occur.

First aid
1. Stop participation.
2. Protect and support the limb.
3. Avoid forceful movement and weight-bearing if painful.
4. Use a wrapped cold pack for comfort.
5. Arrange prompt clinical assessment.
Diagnosis and treatment

Some growth plate injuries are not obvious on an initial X-ray. Persistent focal tenderness must not be dismissed solely because an early image appears normal.

Treatment may include immobilization, reduction, surgery, and follow-up to monitor growth. The approach depends on location, displacement, severity, and remaining growth.

Medicines

Pain relief may be used when appropriate, but no tablet corrects displacement or protects growth by itself.

Prevention and return to sport

Use age-appropriate training loads and take persistent pain seriously. Do not pressure a student to continue because adults would regard the injury as a minor sprain.

Return requires healing, rehabilitation, and clinician guidance. Follow-up may continue after symptoms improve to identify growth disturbance. NIAMS


G. Overuse injuries and training-related pain

1. Delayed-onset muscle soreness — DOMS

What it is

DOMS is muscle soreness that develops after unfamiliar or more demanding exercise, particularly exercise involving lengthening contractions, such as downhill running or lowering weights.

It commonly begins approximately 12–24 hours afterward and is often most noticeable around 24–72 hours. It is not caused by lactic acid remaining in the muscles.

Causes and symptoms

Unaccustomed loading produces muscle changes associated with the repair and adaptation process. Soreness, stiffness, tenderness, and temporarily reduced strength may occur.

DOMS is usually distributed through the exercised muscles. Sudden pain during activity, a localized defect, major bruising, or substantial weakness suggests another injury.

Initial care and treatment
1. Reduce demanding exercise involving the sore muscles.
2. Continue comfortable light movement.
3. Prioritize sleep, food, and appropriate hydration.
4. Resume harder activity progressively as function returns.
Gentle massage or temperature-based comfort measures may help symptoms, but no remedy guarantees immediate recovery.

Medicines

Routine preventive painkillers are unnecessary. Do not take medicine simply to complete another maximal session while movement remains impaired.

Prevention and return

Introduce unfamiliar exercise progressively. Soreness is not a necessary marker of a successful workout.

Return when movement, strength, and coordination are adequate for the task. Severe pain, marked swelling, or dark urine needs urgent assessment rather than being labeled DOMS. acsm.org
2. Shin splints — medial tibial stress syndrome

What it is

Medial tibial stress syndrome causes exercise-related pain along the inner border of the tibia. It is associated with repeated loading of the lower leg.

“Shin pain” is a symptom, however, and not every case is shin splints.

Causes and symptoms

Sudden changes in running distance, intensity, frequency, or jumping load can contribute. Training conditions, footwear, and individual capacity also matter.

Pain is typically spread along a section of the inner shin rather than confined to one tiny point. Persistent focal pain, pain while walking, or rest pain raises concern for bone stress injury.

Initial care
1. Reduce or stop the painful impact activity.
2. Avoid repeated jumping or hopping tests.
3. Use a wrapped cold pack if it improves comfort.
4. Choose pain-free alternative activity only when appropriate.
5. Arrange assessment if symptoms persist or are focal.
Treatment and medicines

Treatment includes workload modification and rehabilitation addressing relevant strength, movement, and training factors.

Painkillers do not establish that the tibia is safe to load. Imaging may be needed when a stress fracture is suspected.

Prevention and return

Increase running and jumping progressively and allow recovery. Return should begin below the workload that provoked symptoms.

Continuing through worsening shin pain can delay diagnosis. A regional meet is not a suitable setting to test whether a suspected bone injury will tolerate one more race. AAOS
3. Bone stress injury or stress fracture

What it is

Bone stress injury occurs when repetitive loading exceeds bone repair capacity. It ranges from an early stress reaction to a stress fracture.

Location matters: some sites have a greater risk of progression, displacement, or delayed healing.

Causes and symptoms

Rapid increases in training, inadequate recovery, low energy availability, and other bone-health factors can contribute.

Pain may initially occur only during exercise, then progress to walking or rest. Localized tenderness is common. Early X-rays may not identify every injury.

Initial care
1. Stop painful running, jumping, or other impact activity.
2. Avoid repeated self-testing.
3. Protect weight-bearing if walking hurts.
4. Arrange clinical assessment.
Treatment and medicines

Treatment may include activity restriction, a boot or crutches, rehabilitation, and investigation of contributing factors. Certain injuries require surgery.

Pain medicine is not permission to continue loading. Calcium or vitamin D supplements should address assessed needs rather than replace adequate nutrition or injury protection.

Prevention and return

Maintain sufficient energy intake, recovery, and progressive training. Recurrent injuries warrant review of nutrition, menstrual health where relevant, and other medical factors.

Return is diagnosis- and site-specific. It may require clinical and imaging evidence of healing, followed by gradual reloading. A general “six-week recovery” promise is inappropriate. AAOS
4. Patellofemoral pain

What it is

Patellofemoral pain usually involves pain around or behind the kneecap during activities that load a bent knee.

It does not automatically mean the kneecap is damaged or “out of place.”

Causes and symptoms

Training-load changes, muscle capacity, movement patterns, and individual anatomy may contribute.

Pain can occur with stairs, squatting, running, jumping, or prolonged sitting. A large acute swelling, a locked knee, or traumatic instability requires assessment for another injury.

Initial care
1. Temporarily reduce the most provocative activities.
2. Adjust depth, repetitions, speed, or training duration.
3. Maintain comfortable activity where possible.
4. Arrange assessment if symptoms persist.
Treatment and medicines

Education and individualized exercise—commonly involving knee and hip strengthening—are central. Selected patients may benefit from taping or foot orthoses as part of a broader plan.

Pain relief may help temporarily but does not replace rehabilitation. A brace is not a universal cure.

Prevention and return

Build capacity gradually and review training demands. Complete rest until every sensation disappears is not always necessary, but repeatedly provoking worsening pain is unhelpful.

Progress according to function and the response during and after exercise. Return should include tolerance of the sport’s running, squatting, landing, and direction changes. AAOS
5. Patellar tendinopathy — jumper’s knee

What it is

Patellar tendinopathy is load-related pain involving the patellar tendon, commonly near its attachment to the lower kneecap.

In a growing student, nearby growth-related disorders can produce similar pain.

Causes and symptoms

Repeated jumping, landing, sprinting, and abrupt workload changes may exceed tendon capacity.

Pain is usually localized and related to tendon-loading tasks. Some athletes feel better after warming up but worsen later; temporary improvement does not prove recovery.

Initial care
1. Reduce provocative jumping and high-speed loading.
2. Avoid repeated maximal tests.
3. Arrange assessment to confirm the pain source.
4. Retain suitable alternative activity when advised.
Treatment and medicines

A common management approach combines load adjustment with progressive strengthening and later jumping or energy-storage exercises.

The precise exercise dose must be individualized. Research does not establish one superior program for every patient.

Painkillers, straps, or injections do not replace rebuilding load tolerance. Injection decisions require specialist consideration.

Prevention and return

Progress jumping volume and intensity rather than suddenly adding repeated maximal efforts.

Return should include adequate strength, repeated jumping tolerance, and an acceptable symptom response afterward. Do not rely only on whether one jump is painless. Persistent symptoms require review, particularly when the diagnosis was never confirmed. pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov
6. Achilles tendinopathy

What it is

Achilles tendinopathy causes load-related pain in the tendon connecting the calf to the heel. Symptoms may occur in the tendon’s middle portion or at its heel attachment.

These locations can require different exercise modifications.

Causes and symptoms

Changes in running, hills, jumping, footwear, and recovery can contribute. Morning stiffness and pain with running or heel raises are common.

A sudden pop or abrupt loss of push-off strength suggests possible rupture and requires prompt assessment.

Initial care
1. Reduce aggravating running or jumping.
2. Avoid aggressive stretching through pain.
3. Seek assessment for persistent symptoms.
4. Use comfortable alternative exercise if appropriate.
Treatment and medicines

Progressive tendon-loading exercise is an important treatment, particularly for confirmed midportion tendinopathy. Complete prolonged rest is not automatically the best approach.

A heel lift or footwear adjustment may help selected patients. Heel drops below a step are not suitable for every presentation, especially some insertional cases.

Pain medicines may improve comfort but do not restore tendon capacity. Steroid injection around the Achilles requires particular caution.

Prevention and return

Progress calf strength, endurance, running, and jumping. Monitor the response later that day and the following morning.

Return depends on restored function and workload tolerance. A warm-up that temporarily reduces pain is not a clearance test. orthopt.org
7. Plantar heel pain or plantar fasciopathy

What it is

Plantar fasciopathy is a common cause of pain beneath the heel associated with the plantar fascia.

“Plantar fasciitis” is a familiar term, although persistent cases are not explained simply by inflammation.

Causes and symptoms

Repeated loading, sudden activity changes, footwear, and individual tissue capacity can contribute.

Pain is often most noticeable with the first steps after sleep or rest. Heel pain in a growing child may instead involve Sever’s disease, while focal persistent pain can suggest a bone injury.

Initial care
1. Reduce provocative impact activity.
2. Use comfortable supportive footwear.
3. Avoid repeatedly walking barefoot on a painful surface.
4. Arrange assessment if persistent or atypical.
Treatment and medicines

Management may include plantar fascia and calf stretching, strengthening, footwear adjustment, and selected taping or orthoses.

Night splints may help some patients. No insert works for every athlete.

Pain medicine can provide short-term relief. Injections and other procedures require a careful discussion of benefits and risks.

Prevention and return

Build walking and running loads gradually. Return should consider first-step pain, daily walking, and tolerance of sport-specific impact.

Do not assume that a heel spur seen on an image explains all symptoms. A diagnosis should come from the whole clinical picture. Plantar Fasciitis
8. Iliotibial band syndrome

What it is

Iliotibial band syndrome commonly causes pain on the outer side of the knee associated with repeated knee movement, particularly running or cycling.

It is not a diagnosis for every outer-knee pain.

Causes and symptoms

Training changes, repeated loading, running conditions, and movement factors may contribute.

Pain often appears after a period of activity and may worsen with continued running. Swelling, locking, trauma, or instability warrants assessment for another cause.

Initial care
1. Reduce the provoking duration or activity.
2. Avoid running through increasing pain.
3. Consider comfortable alternative activity.
4. Arrange assessment for recurrent symptoms.
Treatment and medicines

Rehabilitation may address hip and lower-limb strength, movement control, running demands, and training progression.

Forceful rolling directly over the painful outer knee is unnecessary and may aggravate symptoms. Stretching alone is not a complete treatment.

Pain relief is supportive rather than corrective.

Prevention and return

Increase workload progressively and identify the circumstances in which pain appears.

Return may begin with shorter, easier activity, advancing according to the symptom response. Being able to walk without pain is not equivalent to tolerating race-distance running.

Persistent outer-knee pain requires reassessment rather than repeated labeling as a “tight IT band.” AAOS
9. Osgood–Schlatter disease

What it is

Osgood–Schlatter disease is growth-related pain at the tibial tubercle—the attachment area below the kneecap.

It is associated with traction at a developing tendon attachment, not an infection.

Causes and symptoms

Running, jumping, and repeated quadriceps loading can provoke symptoms during growth.

Localized tenderness, swelling, or a prominent bump below the knee may occur. Kneeling can be painful.

Initial care
1. Reduce painful running and jumping.
2. Avoid pressing repeatedly on the sore area.
3. Use a wrapped cold pack for comfort if helpful.
4. Arrange assessment if the diagnosis is uncertain or function is impaired.
Sudden severe pain or inability to straighten the knee after an explosive movement requires urgent assessment for an acute injury.

Treatment and medicines

Activity modification, appropriate flexibility work, and strengthening may help. A strap or padding benefits some athletes but is not essential for everyone.

Short-term pain medicine may be appropriate after checking suitability. It should not be used to conceal a limp.

Prevention and return

Manage load during growth and respond early to symptoms. Some students can continue modified activity within an agreed plan; others need a greater reduction.

The aim is comfortable function without progressive pain or altered movement. Symptoms can recur while growth continues, and a residual bump may remain after pain settles. AAOS
10. Sever’s disease

What it is

Sever’s disease, or calcaneal apophysitis, is growth-related irritation at the heel’s developing attachment area.

It is a common explanation for activity-related heel pain in growing children, but assessment must consider other causes.

Causes and symptoms

Running, jumping, and traction from the calf–Achilles complex can aggravate the developing heel.

Pain may affect one or both heels. Limping, discomfort after activity, and tenderness are common.

Initial care
1. Reduce painful impact activity.
2. Avoid forcing the child to run through a limp.
3. Use comfortable supportive shoes.
4. Arrange assessment for substantial or persistent symptoms.
Fever, marked swelling, inability to bear weight, or night pain needs review rather than an automatic Sever’s diagnosis.

Treatment and medicines

Activity modification, heel cushioning, selected stretching, and rehabilitation may help. Severe symptoms sometimes require temporary immobilization.

Pain medicine can improve comfort but is not the main treatment.

Prevention and return

Manage running and jumping loads and use suitable footwear. Do not apply an adult Achilles exercise program automatically to a child.

Return should follow comfortable walking and progressive tolerance of running and jumping without a limp or worsening symptoms. Recurrence during growth is possible. AAOS
11. Rotator cuff tendinopathy

What it is

Rotator cuff tendinopathy involves pain associated with loading the shoulder tendons. It can overlap with other causes of shoulder pain.

An imaging finding does not by itself establish the pain source.

Causes and symptoms

Repetitive throwing, serving, swimming, or overhead lifting may exceed current capacity.

Pain with elevation, reaching, or overhead effort can occur. Marked weakness after trauma, instability, or substantial night symptoms needs assessment.

Initial care
1. Reduce provocative overhead volume.
2. Avoid repeated painful strength tests.
3. Maintain comfortable movement.
4. Seek assessment if symptoms persist or follow significant trauma.
Treatment and medicines

Rehabilitation commonly includes graded rotator cuff and shoulder-blade strengthening and adjustment of sport workload.

Temporary pain relief may help, but repeated medication or injections should not replace a loading plan. Surgery is not automatically required.

In younger students, clinicians should consider instability, growth-related injury, and other diagnoses.

Prevention and return

Progress overhead demands, coordinate recovery, and assess technique when relevant.

Return requires suitable movement, strength, endurance, and tolerance of a graded sport-specific program. Being pain-free at rest does not establish readiness for repeated maximal throws. orthoinfo.org
12. Swimmer’s shoulder

What it is

“Swimmer’s shoulder” is an umbrella term, not a single diagnosis. It may involve rotator cuff or biceps tendon pain, instability, and other shoulder problems.

It overlaps with the previous entry rather than representing an entirely separate disease.

Causes and symptoms

High repetition, changes in distance or intensity, fatigue, and individual technique or capacity may contribute.

Pain during a particular stroke, reduced endurance, and altered movement may occur. Persistent pain should not be treated as an unavoidable part of swimming.

Initial care
1. Reduce the provoking stroke or training component.
2. Avoid painful paddles, resistance sets, or dry-land exercises.
3. Notify the coach and medical team.
4. Arrange assessment when symptoms recur.
Treatment and medicines

Treatment follows the actual diagnosis. Rehabilitation may include shoulder strength, endurance, trunk control, and a gradual return to pool workload.

A generic instruction to “strengthen the shoulder” is insufficient without identifying tolerable exercises and progression.

Pain medicines should not be used to complete unchanged painful sessions.

Prevention and return

Review the combined load from pool training, gym work, and competition. Increase distance, intensity, and resistance thoughtfully.

Return should progress through tolerated drills, stroke work, training, and race demands. Symptoms after the session matter as well as symptoms in the water. AOSSM
13. Lateral elbow tendinopathy — tennis elbow

What it is

Lateral elbow tendinopathy involves pain near the outer elbow where forearm extensor tendons attach.

It can occur without playing tennis and is less typical in children than in adults.

Causes and symptoms

Repeated gripping, wrist extension, racquet use, and lifting may contribute.

Pain with gripping or lifting and tenderness over the outer elbow are common. Locking, swelling, or persistent pain in a young athlete warrants assessment for other conditions.

Initial care
1. Reduce provoking grip and lifting loads.
2. Review equipment and technique.
3. Maintain comfortable movement.
4. Arrange assessment for persistent symptoms.
Treatment and medicines

Progressive forearm strengthening and load adjustment are commonly used. A strap or wrist support may help selected patients.

Medication may reduce pain temporarily but does not rebuild capacity. Injection decisions require a discussion of short- and long-term outcomes.

Prevention and return

Progress training volume and grip demands. Address equipment only where it is relevant; changing a racquet alone may not solve the problem.

Return requires tolerating repeated gripping and sport movements, not merely one painless attempt. In students, avoid assigning an adult tendon diagnosis without considering bone, cartilage, or growth-related injury. AAOS
14. Medial elbow tendinopathy — golfer’s elbow

What it is

Medial elbow tendinopathy affects the flexor–pronator tendon attachment on the inner elbow.

Golf is only one possible provoking activity. Throwing, gripping, racquet sport, and lifting may also contribute.

Causes and symptoms

Repeated wrist flexion, forearm rotation, and loading may provoke pain.

Tenderness and pain with gripping or resisted wrist movements are common. Numbness in the ring and little fingers may indicate nerve involvement.

Initial care
1. Reduce provoking gripping and throwing.
2. Avoid repeatedly stressing the painful elbow.
3. Maintain comfortable motion.
4. Seek assessment for persistent symptoms or tingling.
Treatment and medicines

Treatment commonly includes load modification and progressive rehabilitation. A clinician determines whether tendon, ligament, nerve, or bone pathology is present.

Pain medicine cannot distinguish these problems or make repeated throwing safe.

Prevention and return

Progress grip and throwing loads and address relevant strength and technique factors.

Inner-elbow pain in a growing thrower must not automatically be called golfer’s elbow. A developing attachment site may be injured instead.

Return requires appropriate strength, movement, and tolerance of progressive sport-specific loading without neurological symptoms or worsening pain. AAOS
15. Throwing-related elbow growth plate irritation — Little League elbow

What it is

Little League elbow commonly involves repetitive stress at the developing attachment area on the inner elbow.

The term can encompass related problems, so assessment should establish the structure involved.

Causes and symptoms

Repeated throwing without sufficient recovery can overload immature tissues. Participation across several teams or positions can hide the total workload.

Inner-elbow pain, reduced throwing performance, stiffness, and tenderness may occur.

Initial care
1. Stop painful throwing.
2. Do not switch to another throwing role to avoid resting.
3. Avoid forceful stretching or testing.
4. Arrange clinical assessment.
A sudden pop, substantial swelling, or loss of function may indicate an avulsion fracture and needs prompt assessment.

Treatment and medicines

Treatment may involve a period away from throwing, selected immobilization, and rehabilitation of the elbow, shoulder, trunk, and lower body.

Painkillers should not be used to continue throwing. Significant injuries may require specialist care.

Prevention and return

Follow age-appropriate pitch and rest guidance, count all throwing exposure, and stop when pain or unusual fatigue appears.

Return uses a clinician-directed interval throwing progression after recovery. Reduced pain after a few days is not sufficient clearance. Growth-related injuries deserve protection even during an important meet. HealthyChildren.org
16. Wrist overuse injury, including gymnast’s wrist

What it is

Wrist overuse pain can arise from tendons, cartilage, ligaments, or bone. In growing gymnasts, “gymnast’s wrist” commonly refers to stress injury of the distal radial growth plate.

It is not simply a flexibility problem.

Causes and symptoms

Repeated weight-bearing through an extended wrist can overload developing tissues.

Pain during handstands, vaulting, push-ups, or similar loading may occur. Persistent focal tenderness and reduced movement need assessment.

Initial care
1. Stop painful wrist weight-bearing.
2. Do not repeatedly perform handstands to test it.
3. Support the wrist if advised.
4. Arrange clinical assessment.
Treatment and medicines

A growth plate injury may require immobilization and a substantial change in training. Other diagnoses require different management.

Pain medicine does not protect the growth plate. Continuing with a brace is not automatically safe.

Prevention and return

Progress upper-limb loading, review technique and equipment, and allow recovery. Pain should not be dismissed as normal gymnastics training.

Return requires appropriate clinical recovery and graded loading. Some injuries need follow-up beyond symptom resolution to check growth and alignment. HealthyChildren.org
17. Bursitis

What it is

Bursitis is irritation or inflammation of a bursa, a small fluid-containing structure that reduces friction between tissues.

It can occur near the knee, elbow, shoulder, hip, or heel.

Causes and symptoms

Repeated pressure, friction, direct trauma, inflammatory disease, or infection may cause it.

Localized tenderness, swelling, and pain with pressure or movement can occur. Not every swelling near a joint is bursitis.

Initial care
1. Reduce pressure and provocative activity.
2. Protect the area with suitable padding.
3. Use a wrapped cold pack if helpful.
4. Seek assessment if swelling is substantial or persistent.
A hot, rapidly swollen area, fever, a skin wound, or feeling unwell raises concern for infection. Do not attempt drainage.

Treatment and medicines

Noninfectious cases may receive activity modification, rehabilitation, and suitable pain relief.

Suspected infection may require fluid sampling and antibiotics. Steroid injection is not appropriate when infection is present and is not automatically necessary for noninfectious bursitis.

Prevention and return

Use protective padding, avoid prolonged pressure, and address the provoking workload.

Return depends on cause, symptoms, skin condition, and ability to perform safely. Infection requires clinical management rather than a simple “rest and ice” plan. NHS
18. Lumbar bone stress injury or spondylolysis

What it is

Spondylolysis involves a defect or stress injury in part of a vertebra, commonly the pars interarticularis. It is an important cause of back pain in young athletes.

It differs from an uncomplicated muscle strain. Some cases are associated with vertebral slipping, called spondylolisthesis.

Causes and symptoms

Repeated back extension and rotation can contribute, particularly in activities such as gymnastics, throwing, and weightlifting.

Pain may worsen with extension or sport-specific loading. Symptoms alone do not confirm the diagnosis.

Initial care
1. Stop movements that repeatedly provoke pain.
2. Avoid back bends or repeated extension testing.
3. Arrange clinical assessment.
4. Seek emergency care for new bladder or bowel dysfunction, groin numbness, or significant leg weakness.
Treatment and medicines

Management may involve activity restriction, rehabilitation, and selected bracing. Imaging decisions depend on the clinical presentation.

Pain medicine does not establish that the bone can safely tolerate loading. Persistent adolescent back pain should not be repeatedly treated as soreness without evaluation.

Prevention and return

Progress extension and rotational demands and address strength, technique, and recovery.

Return is individualized and may require specialist follow-up. The athlete should progress through rehabilitation and sport tasks under clinical guidance rather than return simply because ordinary walking is comfortable. AAOS

H. Head, face, eye, ear, and dental problems

1. Concussion

What the condition is

A concussion is a traumatic brain injury caused by forces transmitted to the brain. It can follow a direct head impact or a blow to the body that rapidly moves the head.

Loss of consciousness is not required. A student may remain awake, speak normally, and still have a concussion.

Main causes and why it happens during sport

Falls, collisions, ball impacts, tackles, and contact with equipment or playing surfaces can cause concussion. Symptoms may be immediate or develop over the following hours.

A helmet can protect against certain injuries, but wearing one does not rule out concussion.

Common symptoms

Headache, dizziness, nausea, blurred vision, light or noise sensitivity, balance problems, slowed thinking, confusion, memory difficulty, and unusual behavior may occur.

The athlete may appear dazed, answer slowly, become unsteady, or report feeling “not right.” Symptoms must not be dismissed because the student wants to continue. CDC

Step-by-step first aid
1. Remove the athlete from participation immediately.
2. Check breathing, responsiveness, and emergency warning signs.
3. Avoid unnecessary movement if neck injury is possible.
4. Keep the athlete supervised.
5. Arrange medical assessment.
6. Inform the parent or guardian and relevant medical personnel.
7. Record the mechanism, symptoms, and any loss of consciousness.
No same-day return to sport is permitted when concussion is suspected, even if symptoms improve.

Treatment and medicines

There is no medicine that instantly cures concussion. Treatment includes individualized symptom management and a supported return to learning and activity.

During the first day or two, reduce demanding activities as advised, but prolonged isolation in a dark room or complete bed rest is not recommended. Light activity may be introduced within clinical guidance.

Ask the treating clinician about pain medicines; do not use them to conceal symptoms for clearance. CDC

Prevention and return to sport

Promote safe technique, rule enforcement, appropriate equipment, and honest symptom reporting.

Return requires healthcare-provider approval and a graded progression. CDC’s progression typically allows at least 24 hours per stage, with monitoring for symptom recurrence. Recovery is individualized; it is not a guaranteed one-week process. CDC
2. Nosebleed, including after impact

What the condition is

A nosebleed occurs when blood vessels inside the nose bleed. Many arise near the front of the nasal septum, where vessels are easily irritated.

A nosebleed after an impact also requires assessment for nasal, facial, and head injury.

Main causes

Dryness, nose picking, forceful blowing, irritation, and trauma are common causes. Certain medicines and bleeding disorders can increase risk or prolong bleeding.

Common symptoms

Blood may come from one or both nostrils. Swallowed blood can cause nausea or vomiting.

Heavy bleeding, weakness, dizziness, or breathing difficulty requires urgent care.

Step-by-step first aid
1. Sit the athlete upright.
2. Lean the head forward.
3. Pinch the soft part of the nose just above the nostrils.
4. Maintain uninterrupted pressure for 10–15 minutes.
5. Breathe through the mouth and spit out blood rather than swallowing it.
6. Seek urgent care if bleeding persists or is heavy.
Do not tilt the head backward or repeatedly release pressure to check. Do not stuff loose tissue deep into the nostrils.

Treatment and medicines

A clinician may use cautery, an appropriate topical treatment, or nasal packing. These should not be improvised at the sideline.

A cold pack may provide comfort but does not replace direct pressure.

Prevention and return to sport

Avoid picking and forceful blowing. Recurrent episodes deserve assessment.

After bleeding stops, avoid strenuous exercise and heavy lifting for approximately 24 hours, following medical advice. An impact-related nosebleed requires assessment of the associated injury before return. NHS
3. Nasal injury or suspected nasal fracture

What the injury is

A nasal injury can affect the nasal bones, cartilage, septum, or nearby facial structures. Swelling alone cannot determine whether the nose is fractured.

A septal hematoma is a collection of blood within the nasal septum. It requires urgent treatment because delayed care can damage cartilage.

Main causes and symptoms

A ball, elbow, fall, or collision may cause injury. Pain, tenderness, swelling, bruising, bleeding, deformity, or blocked nasal breathing may occur.

A nose that looks straight can still have an important internal injury.

Step-by-step first aid
1. Stop participation.
2. Assess for concussion and other facial injuries.
3. Control bleeding using forward leaning and soft-nose pressure.
4. Apply a wrapped cold pack gently.
5. Do not attempt to straighten the nose.
6. Arrange clinical assessment.
New or worsening nasal blockage after injury, particularly with internal swelling, needs urgent evaluation for septal hematoma. Clear drainage after substantial trauma, vision problems, or severe headache also requires urgent care. rch.org.au

Treatment and medicines

Pain relief may be appropriate after checking suitability. A clinician determines whether specialist review or realignment is needed.

Assessment may be repeated after swelling decreases, but suspected septal hematoma must not wait for swelling to settle. Not every isolated nasal fracture requires an X-ray; investigation depends on the suspected injuries.

Prevention and return to sport

Use sport-appropriate face protection and safe technique.

Return depends on the injury, healing, breathing, and risk of another impact. A protective mask does not automatically establish fitness to compete. Obtain medical advice rather than allowing return solely because bleeding has stopped. rch.org.au
4. Lip or mouth cuts

What the injury is

Cuts can affect the lips, tongue, gums, or inner cheeks. The mouth has a rich blood supply, so a small wound can bleed noticeably.

Some injuries extend through the lip or involve teeth, jawbone, or embedded tooth fragments.

Main causes and symptoms

An athlete may bite the lip or tongue during a fall or impact. Contact with braces, another player, or equipment can also cause injury.

Pain, swelling, bleeding, and difficulty eating may occur. A cut crossing the border between the red lip and surrounding skin needs careful assessment because accurate alignment affects healing and appearance.

Step-by-step first aid
1. Stop activity and check breathing.
2. Use gloves if available.
3. Apply steady pressure with clean gauze where safely accessible.
4. If fully alert, gently rinse away loose blood or debris with clean water.
5. Apply a wrapped cold pack externally for swelling.
6. Assess the teeth and jaw.
7. Seek care for persistent bleeding, a deep or gaping wound, or suspected embedded material.
Do not leave loose gauze in the mouth of a drowsy child. HealthyChildren.org

Treatment and medicines

Many small inner-mouth cuts heal without sutures. Deep, through-and-through, significantly gaping, or lip-border injuries may need repair.

Suitable pain relief can help. Antibiotics are not automatic for every mouth cut; the clinician considers contamination, wound type, and associated injury.

Do not use skin glue inside the mouth or apply harsh disinfectants.

Prevention and return to sport

Use a properly fitted mouthguard for activities with dental and facial injury risk.

Return requires controlled bleeding, safe swallowing, acceptable pain, and protection against reopening. Dental damage, concussion, or jaw injury requires its own assessment. HealthyChildren.org
5. Chipped, fractured, loosened, or knocked-out tooth

What the injury is

Dental trauma can damage the crown, root, supporting tissues, or surrounding bone. A tooth may be chipped, displaced, loosened, pushed into the socket, or completely knocked out.

A knocked-out permanent tooth is a time-critical dental emergency. A knocked-out baby tooth must not be replanted.

Main causes and symptoms

Falls, collisions, balls, and equipment impacts are common causes. Pain, bleeding, altered tooth position, sensitivity, or an abnormal bite may occur.

Little pain does not exclude significant injury.

Immediate priorities
1. Assess breathing, consciousness, and associated head or facial injury.
2. Contact emergency dental care immediately.
3. Handle a recovered tooth by the crown—the visible chewing part—not the root.
4. Do not scrub, scrape, disinfect, or dry the root.
For a knocked-out permanent tooth

If the person is alert and cooperative, the tooth is clearly permanent, and replantation can be performed safely by a capable first aider, prompt replacement into the socket offers the best opportunity for preservation. If dirty, rinse gently without scrubbing.

Do not force it. If replantation is unsafe, uncertain, or unsuccessful, place the tooth in milk or an appropriate tooth-preservation solution and obtain immediate dental care. Do not let it dry out.

Do not store a tooth loose inside a child’s mouth because it can be swallowed or inhaled. aapd.org

For other dental injuries

Save a broken fragment if available and take it to the dentist. Do not repeatedly wiggle a loose tooth or attempt to reposition one pushed into the gum.

If a tooth is missing and cannot be found, tell the medical team. Breathing symptoms require emergency assessment for possible aspiration.

Treatment and medicines

Treatment may involve bonding, repositioning, splinting, pulp treatment, or other procedures. A dentist decides whether antibiotics, mouth rinse, or tetanus review is indicated.

Pain medicine does not replace urgent dental treatment.

Prevention and return to sport

Use a well-fitting mouthguard. Return depends on the dental injury, stabilization, associated injuries, and dentist’s instructions—not simply whether bleeding stops. digitaleditions.walsworth.com
6. Eye irritation from dust, sweat, or pool chemicals

What the condition is

Minor irritants can disturb the eye surface and cause stinging, watering, and redness. Pool-related irritation can involve water chemistry and airborne or waterborne irritants.

However, an irritated-looking eye may also have a scratch, retained foreign body, infection, or chemical burn.

Common symptoms

Burning, grittiness, tearing, redness, and temporary discomfort may occur. Persistent blurred vision, significant pain, or light sensitivity is not typical of a trivial irritation and needs assessment.

Step-by-step first aid
1. Stop activity.
2. Do not rub the eye.
3. Begin gentle irrigation with clean, lukewarm or cool water.
4. Remove contact lenses if easily possible, without delaying irrigation.
5. Avoid a forceful water jet.
6. Obtain care if symptoms persist or vision is affected.
For significant chemical exposure, irrigate immediately and continuously for at least 20 minutes while arranging emergency advice. Take the product container or safety information when safe. Do not try to neutralize one chemical with another.

Treatment and medicines

Minor irritation may settle after rinsing. A clinician or pharmacist may recommend a suitable lubricating drop once a more serious problem is excluded.

Do not use leftover antibiotic, steroid, anesthetic, or “redness-removing” drops to hide symptoms. NHS

Prevention and return to sport

Use appropriate sports eye protection or correctly fitted swimming goggles. Follow pool safety and chemical-handling procedures.

Return only after normal vision and comfortable eye function have returned. Ongoing pain, redness, or visual disturbance requires assessment before swimming or ball sports. Contact lenses should not be worn while the eye remains irritated or injured. NHS
7. Eye foreign body or corneal abrasion

What the condition is

A foreign body is material on or within the eye. A corneal abrasion is a scratch of the clear front surface—the cornea.

Dust, grit, fingernails, equipment, and contact lenses can cause injury. A penetrating injury is different from a superficial particle and is an emergency.

Common symptoms

Pain, tearing, a persistent foreign-body sensation, difficulty opening the eye, light sensitivity, and blurred vision may occur.

A particle may have left the eye while a scratch continues to cause symptoms.

Step-by-step first aid
1. Stop activity.
2. Do not rub or press on the eye.
3. For a loose superficial particle, gently rinse with clean water or sterile saline.
4. Do not use tweezers, cotton buds, or fingers to scrape the eye.
5. Arrange prompt assessment for persistent symptoms.
If an object is embedded or the eye may be penetrated, do not remove it or irrigate as though it were ordinary dust. Avoid pressure, protect the eye with a rigid shield if available, and seek emergency care.

High-speed impacts and vision changes require urgent assessment even if the visible injury looks small. American Academy of Ophthalmology

Treatment and medicines

An eye professional may examine the eye with fluorescein dye and magnification, remove a foreign body, and prescribe treatment when appropriate.

Antibiotic selection differs for contact-lens-related injuries. Do not self-use steroid or numbing drops. Routine pressure patching is not a substitute for proper assessment.

Prevention and return to sport

Wear suitable impact-rated protection where indicated. Ordinary spectacles or contact lenses are not equivalent to sports eye protection.

Return requires adequate healing, normal vision, and clearance where appropriate. Avoid contact lenses until instructed that they are safe. American Academy of Ophthalmology
8. Conjunctivitis

What the condition is

Conjunctivitis is inflammation of the conjunctiva, the thin tissue covering the white of the eye and inner eyelids.

Causes include viruses, bacteria, allergies, and irritants. Infectious forms may spread; allergic conjunctivitis is not contagious.

Main causes and symptoms

Redness, watering, grittiness, itching, and discharge may occur. Allergy often causes prominent itching, while bacterial infection can cause sticky discharge. These patterns are helpful but do not reliably establish the cause by themselves.

Significant pain, light sensitivity, or persistent vision change is a warning sign for another eye problem.

Initial care
1. Wash hands before and after touching the face.
2. Avoid rubbing the eyes.
3. Gently clean discharge with clean water and a fresh pad for each eye.
4. Use a clean cool compress for comfort.
5. Stop contact-lens use.
6. Avoid sharing towels, pillowcases, goggles, or eye products.
Treatment and medicines

Treatment depends on the cause:
- Viral conjunctivitis usually receives supportive care.
- Selected bacterial cases may need antibiotic drops or ointment.
- Allergic conjunctivitis may benefit from appropriate antihistamine or other allergy treatment.
Antibiotics do not treat viral or allergic conjunctivitis. Steroid eye drops require appropriate specialist supervision. nhs.uk

When to seek medical care

Seek prompt assessment for eye pain, marked light sensitivity, vision changes, severe redness, or a red painful eye in a contact-lens wearer. Do not wait for presumed “pink eye” to resolve if these features are present.

Prevention and return to sport

Use careful hygiene and avoid shared personal items. School attendance guidance does not automatically settle close-contact sports participation.

Return depends on the cause, comfort, vision, ability to maintain hygiene, and event requirements. Do not prescribe an arbitrary “24 hours of antibiotics” rule for every red eye, because many cases do not need antibiotics at all. nhs.uk
9. Swimmer’s ear — otitis externa

What the condition is

Otitis externa affects the outer ear canal. It differs from a middle-ear infection behind the eardrum.

Water remaining in the canal can contribute to irritation and infection. Bacteria are common causes; fungal infection or underlying dermatitis can also be involved.

Main causes and symptoms

Swimming, canal trauma from cotton buds, irritating ear devices, and skin conditions may contribute.

Pain, itching, a blocked sensation, discharge, and reduced hearing can occur. Tenderness when moving the outer ear or pressing near the canal opening is suggestive but does not replace examination.

Initial care
1. Stop swimming.
2. Keep the ear dry.
3. Wipe discharge only from the outside.
4. Do not insert cotton buds, fingers, or instruments.
5. Arrange assessment for pain, discharge, or hearing change.
Fever, spreading swelling, severe pain, dizziness, or significant illness requires prompt care. Diabetes or immune suppression increases the need for careful assessment. NHS

Treatment and medicines

A clinician may prescribe antibiotic ear drops, sometimes combined with an anti-inflammatory ingredient. Fungal disease requires different treatment.

A markedly swollen canal may need professional cleaning or a wick to help drops reach the affected area.

Do not use homemade alcohol, vinegar, peroxide, or drying drops when there is pain, discharge, an eardrum perforation, ear tubes, or uncertainty about the eardrum. Product selection must account for eardrum status.

Suitable pain medicine may help; oral antibiotics are not routine for every uncomplicated case. CDC

Prevention and return to sport

Dry the outer ears gently after swimming. Consider well-fitted swim protection if tolerated and recommended. Do not remove protective earwax aggressively.

Return to swimming should follow symptom recovery and clinical advice. Earplugs do not automatically make active infection safe for competition. CDC


I. Nutrition, menstrual health, recovery, and psychological concerns

1. Iron deficiency

What the condition is

Iron deficiency means the body has insufficient iron stores or available iron for its needs. It can occur without anemia, meaning hemoglobin has not yet fallen below the appropriate reference range.

Iron supports oxygen transport, energy metabolism, and other functions. Low stores may affect health and training response before anemia develops.

Main causes and contributing factors

Possible causes include inadequate intake, insufficient overall fueling, menstrual blood loss, other bleeding, increased requirements during growth, and impaired absorption.

The cause must be investigated rather than assuming every athlete simply needs more meat or a supplement.

Symptoms and diagnosis

Fatigue, reduced endurance, difficulty concentrating, and poor training response may occur, but are nonspecific.

Assessment may include a blood count, ferritin, transferrin saturation, and other tests. Ferritin can rise during inflammation, so one result needs clinical interpretation.

Initial action and treatment
1. Reduce demanding activity if unusually weak or symptomatic.
2. Arrange medical assessment.
3. Review food intake with a qualified dietitian.
4. Investigate blood loss or absorption problems when indicated.
Treatment may involve dietary changes and prescribed oral iron. Intravenous iron is reserved for selected clinical circumstances.

Medicine precautions

Do not self-prescribe high-dose iron or use injections as a performance shortcut. Excess iron can be harmful, and supplements can cause gastrointestinal effects.

Prevention and return to sport

Maintain sufficient overall intake and varied iron-containing foods. Plant-based diets can support athletes but require appropriate planning.

Participation depends on symptoms, test results, and the underlying cause. Improvement should be monitored rather than judged only from perceived energy. ASC
2. Iron-deficiency anemia

What the condition is

Iron-deficiency anemia occurs when inadequate iron contributes to reduced hemoglobin. Hemoglobin carries oxygen in red blood cells.

It is a more advanced problem than low iron stores alone. However, not all anemia is caused by iron deficiency.

Main causes and symptoms

Inadequate intake, blood loss, and absorption problems are important causes. Heavy menstrual bleeding may contribute and needs attention in its own right.

Symptoms may include fatigue, reduced exercise tolerance, headache, dizziness, palpitations, and breathlessness. These symptoms do not establish the diagnosis.

Initial action
1. Stop strenuous exercise if dizzy, unusually breathless, or weak.
2. Arrange medical assessment and appropriate blood tests.
3. Seek urgent help for fainting, chest pain, or significant breathing difficulty.
4. Investigate the cause rather than treating the blood result alone.
Treatment and medicines

Oral iron is commonly used when appropriate. The formulation, dose, schedule, and duration should be selected for the individual.

Some patients require intravenous treatment or other care. Severe symptomatic anemia needs urgent evaluation; a transfusion is not a routine sports treatment.

Iron is often continued beyond the initial improvement in hemoglobin to restore stores, with follow-up testing.

Prevention and return to sport

Address the underlying dietary or medical problem. Do not assume that eating one iron-rich meal will quickly correct anemia.

Return to demanding exercise depends on severity, symptoms, treatment response, and clinical advice. An athlete should not be pressured to compete while significantly symptomatic. NHLBI, NIH
3. Low blood glucose — hypoglycemia

What the condition is

Hypoglycemia is blood glucose low enough to require action. In diabetes care, a value below approximately **70 mg/dL—3.9 mmol/L—**is an important treatment threshold.

Severe hypoglycemia means the person needs assistance because of impaired functioning, regardless of the measured number.

Main causes

Risk is particularly important in athletes using insulin or certain glucose-lowering medicines. Exercise, delayed meals, and a mismatch between medication and activity can contribute.

Symptoms resembling hypoglycemia can have other causes. Recurrent episodes in someone without diabetes require assessment.

Symptoms

Shaking, sweating, hunger, palpitations, dizziness, irritability, confusion, and reduced coordination may occur.

First aid
1. Stop activity and check glucose when possible.
2. Follow the athlete’s individual diabetes plan.
3. If alert and able to swallow, give fast-acting carbohydrate.
4. For many adolescents and adults, the usual approach is 15 grams, wait 15 minutes, then recheck.
5. Repeat according to the plan if still low.
6. After recovery, provide a suitable snack or meal when needed.
Younger children may need a smaller amount. Their prescribed plan takes priority.

For reduced consciousness, seizure, or unsafe swallowing: activate emergency help and give prescribed glucagon if available and someone knows how to administer it. Do not put glucose gel into an unconscious person’s mouth. American Diabetes Association

Prevention and return to sport

Plan food, glucose monitoring, medication adjustments, and emergency supplies with the diabetes team. Exercise-related lows can occur later, not only during the event.

Do not immediately resume competition after one improved reading. Confirm recovery, identify the cause, and follow the individual plan.
4. Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport — REDs

What the condition is

REDs is a syndrome of impaired health and performance associated with problematic low energy availability: insufficient dietary energy remaining to support normal body functions after exercise demands.

It can affect athletes of any sex and body size. It may occur unintentionally and does not always involve an eating disorder.

Main causes and contributing factors

Training increases without adequate fueling, limited meal access, demanding schedules, nutrition misunderstandings, and restrictive eating can contribute.

In growing students, food also needs to support growth and development—not just training.

Possible signs

Persistent fatigue, reduced training response, recurrent illness, bone stress injuries, menstrual disturbance, mood changes, or disrupted growth may raise concern.

No single symptom or body measurement confirms REDs.

Initial action and treatment
1. Arrange medical and sports-dietitian assessment.
2. Review training and food access supportively.
3. Reduce activity where health or injury risk requires it.
4. Address psychological concerns when present.
Treatment generally involves restoring adequate fueling and, when necessary, modifying exercise. Associated bone, hormonal, and other medical problems may need treatment.

Medicines

There is no tablet that replaces adequate energy availability. Vitamins, protein powders, or hormonal medicines alone do not correct the underlying problem.

Prevention and return to sport

Provide regular opportunities to eat, adequate recovery, and a culture that avoids appearance-based pressure.

Return-to-sport decisions are individualized and may involve restrictions while medical risks improve. A normal appearance or strong performance does not establish medical safety. ASC
5. Disordered eating or eating disorders

What the concern is

Disordered eating describes problematic eating-related behaviors that may not meet criteria for a specific disorder. Eating disorders are serious, treatable mental illnesses—not choices or failures of discipline.

They can occur at any body size and may be medically serious even when the athlete appears healthy.

Causes and warning signs

Biological, psychological, and social factors interact. Sport-related pressures may contribute but are not the only explanation.

Warning signs can include rigid food rules, distress around meals, compulsive exercise, avoidance of eating with others, recurrent dizziness, and increasing preoccupation with body shape or performance.

Supportive initial response
1. Speak privately and without judgment.
2. Express concern about health and well-being.
3. Avoid comments about appearance or praising restriction.
4. Help arrange medical and mental-health assessment.
5. Involve a parent, guardian, or trusted adult appropriately for a minor.
6. Obtain urgent care for fainting, severe weakness, dehydration, or other medical instability.
Treatment and medicines

Treatment may involve medical monitoring, psychological therapy, nutritional rehabilitation, and family support. Some patients need hospital care.

Medicines may help selected disorders or associated conditions, but do not replace comprehensive treatment.

Significant undernutrition may require medically supervised nutritional restoration.

Prevention and return to sport

Avoid public weigh-ins, body shaming, and unsupervised weight manipulation. Support confidential access to care.

Participation depends on medical and psychological safety, not willingness to compete. Recovery is possible, and the aim is health rather than rapid clearance. National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH)
6. Menstrual irregularity associated with low energy availability

What the condition is

Low energy availability can disrupt hormonal signaling and contribute to infrequent or absent periods. One possible diagnosis is functional hypothalamic amenorrhea.

Missing periods is not a normal achievement of athletic training. However, not every irregular cycle is caused by sport or underfueling.

Other causes and assessment

Pregnancy, thyroid disease, polycystic ovary syndrome, medicines, contraception, and other conditions must be considered.

Adolescent cycles can vary, but persistent changes deserve assessment. Seek advice for three missed periods, no first period by age 15, or earlier when there are other concerns.

Initial action
1. Arrange a confidential medical discussion.
2. Review menstrual history, nutrition, training, stress, and medicines.
3. Assess possible pregnancy when relevant.
4. Investigate associated bone pain or stress injuries.
Treatment and medicines

When low energy availability is responsible, treatment focuses on adequate fueling, appropriate exercise adjustment, and psychological support where needed.

A contraceptive pill may produce withdrawal bleeding without showing that natural hormonal function has recovered. It should not be prescribed solely to “restart periods” or fix bone health in this condition.

Other hormonal treatment may be considered by a specialist in selected cases.

Prevention and return to sport

Track changes privately if helpful, without making menstrual information a public team matter.

Participation is individualized. Menstrual recovery is important, but it is one part of broader health assessment rather than the sole clearance criterion. NHS
7. Menstrual cramps — dysmenorrhea

What the condition is

Primary dysmenorrhea is menstrual pain associated with uterine contractions and prostaglandins, without an identified underlying pelvic disorder.

Secondary dysmenorrhea results from another condition, such as endometriosis.

Symptoms

Lower abdominal cramps may spread to the back or thighs. Nausea, headache, fatigue, or bowel changes can accompany them.

Severe, progressively worsening, unusual, or activity-limiting pain should not simply be dismissed as normal menstruation.

Initial care
1. Offer privacy, toilet access, and menstrual supplies.
2. Allow rest or modified activity.
3. Apply a comfortably warm, wrapped heat pack.
4. Encourage normal food and fluids.
5. Arrange assessment for unusual or severe symptoms.
Treatment and medicines

An NSAID such as ibuprofen can help when suitable. Paracetamol is an alternative for some patients. Do not combine multiple NSAIDs or use them without checking age, hydration, allergies, and medical history.

Clinician-directed hormonal treatment can reduce pain for some people.

Urgent warning signs

Sudden severe or one-sided pelvic pain, fainting, fever, or pain with possible pregnancy needs urgent assessment.

Prevention and return to sport

A symptom record can help identify patterns and plan treatment. Comfortable activity may help some people, but no athlete should be forced to exercise through disabling pain.

Return depends on comfort and function; menstruation itself does not require exclusion from sport. nhs.uk
8. Sleep deprivation or sleep disturbance

What the concern is

Sleep deprivation means inadequate sleep opportunity or duration. Sleep disorders involve problems such as persistent insomnia, abnormal breathing during sleep, or disrupted timing.

Fatigue and poor concentration may result, but these symptoms can also reflect other conditions.

Main causes

Early training, late travel, homework, anxiety, noisy accommodation, caffeine, and inconsistent schedules can interfere with sleep.

Adolescents aged approximately 13–18 generally need 8–10 hours per 24 hours. Younger school-aged children usually need more. Individual recovery demands also matter.

Initial action
1. Review the schedule and actual sleep opportunity.
2. Reduce hazardous or technically demanding activity if excessively sleepy.
3. Provide a quiet, comfortable sleep environment.
4. Maintain regular sleep and wake times where practical.
5. Seek assessment for persistent problems, loud snoring, breathing pauses, or substantial daytime sleepiness.
Treatment and medicines

Persistent insomnia may benefit from structured behavioral treatment. Sleeping tablets, sedating antihistamines, and melatonin should not be routine self-treatment for student-athletes.

Energy drinks do not replace sleep and can perpetuate sleep problems.

Prevention and return to sport

Plan travel and training around recovery, not only competition time. Avoid making inadequate sleep a sign of commitment.

Readiness should consider alertness, reaction time, and coordination. A student struggling to stay awake should not perform activities where a lapse could cause injury. pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov
9. Competition-related anxiety

What the concern is

Some nervousness before an event is common. Anxiety becomes a concern when distress is excessive, persistent, or interferes with functioning.

It does not mean that an athlete is weak or unprepared.

Causes and symptoms

Performance expectations, fear of mistakes, selection pressure, previous experiences, and wider life stress may contribute.

Symptoms can include racing thoughts, tension, stomach discomfort, disturbed sleep, avoidance, palpitations, and difficulty concentrating. Physical symptoms should not automatically be assumed to be anxiety.

Immediate support
1. Move to a quieter space if the athlete wants this.
2. Listen without criticism.
3. Use calm, simple communication.
4. Help focus on manageable immediate actions.
5. Encourage comfortable breathing rather than forced deep breaths.
6. Arrange professional support if symptoms recur or interfere with daily life.
Treatment and medicines

Psychological approaches, including cognitive behavioral techniques, can help. Treatment should address the athlete’s needs, not simply make participation possible.

Medication may be appropriate for a diagnosed condition under clinical care. Sedatives or borrowed “calming tablets” are unsafe solutions.

Prevention and participation

Use realistic process goals, supportive coaching, predictable routines, and permission to report distress.

Participation should be collaborative. Avoid both forcing an overwhelmed athlete to compete and assuming that all anxiety requires withdrawal. Persistent distress needs assessment and a plan. NHS
10. Panic attacks

What the condition is

A panic attack is a sudden episode of intense fear or distress accompanied by physical symptoms. One attack does not automatically establish panic disorder.

Panic disorder involves recurrent unexpected attacks and associated ongoing concern or behavior changes, assessed clinically.

Symptoms and important distinctions

Palpitations, shaking, breathlessness, dizziness, tingling, chest discomfort, and fear of losing control may occur.

Asthma, hypoglycemia, heat illness, cardiac problems, and other conditions can resemble panic. A first or atypical episode during exercise deserves medical assessment.

Immediate support
1. Stop activity and assess emergency warning signs.
2. Stay with the person.
3. Speak calmly and ask what usually helps.
4. Allow a comfortable position and gentle breathing.
5. Use grounding techniques if welcomed.
6. Obtain medical help for uncertainty, persistent symptoms, or deterioration.
Do not use paper-bag rebreathing. Do not dismiss chest pain or collapse as panic because the person has had anxiety before.

Treatment and medicines

CBT is an established treatment for panic disorder. A clinician may consider medicines for selected patients, taking age and diagnosis into account.

Do not give someone else’s medicine or use sedating treatment to return the athlete immediately to competition.

Prevention and return

Develop an individualized coping and treatment plan. Return follows recovery, assessment of safety, and the athlete’s readiness.

The goal is confidence and effective treatment—not blame or avoidance of all physical activity. National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH)
11. Athlete burnout

What the concern is

Burnout involves persistent physical or emotional exhaustion, reduced sense of accomplishment, and growing detachment or loss of enjoyment in sport.

It differs from one disappointing performance or ordinary tiredness after a hard session.

Contributing factors

Continuous competition, excessive specialization, limited control, pressure, inadequate recovery, and conflict between sport and other life demands can contribute.

Symptoms may include dread of training, irritability, declining motivation, withdrawal, and feeling that participation is an obligation rather than a choice.

Initial response
1. Listen privately and take the concern seriously.
2. Avoid calling the athlete lazy or ungrateful.
3. Review workload across school, travel, and sport.
4. Provide meaningful rest and choice.
5. Assess for injury, depression, anxiety, or other health concerns.
6. Arrange professional support when needed.
Treatment and medicines

Management may involve schedule changes, time away from sport, supportive counseling, and changes in the training environment.

There is no specific medicine that cures burnout. Associated mental-health conditions may require their own treatment.

Prevention and return

Protect enjoyment, autonomy, recovery, and life outside sport. AAP guidance supports regular weekly breaks and time away from a particular sport during the year.

Return should reflect the athlete’s health and preferences. A break should not be used as punishment, and leaving one sport does not mean abandoning physical activity. HealthyChildren.org
12. Overtraining syndrome

What the condition is

Overtraining syndrome involves a prolonged maladaptive response associated with training and other stressors, inadequate recovery, and persistent performance impairment.

Ordinary fatigue alone does not establish it. Short-term training fatigue, nonfunctional overreaching, burnout, REDs, and illness can overlap but are not identical.

Symptoms and diagnosis

Persistent performance decline, fatigue, disturbed sleep, mood changes, and poor recovery may occur.

There is no single blood test, heart-rate measurement, or wearable score that confirms the syndrome. Assessment excludes alternative explanations such as iron deficiency, infection, inadequate fueling, sleep disorders, and psychological illness.

Initial action
1. Reduce or stop demanding training.
2. Arrange clinical assessment.
3. Review total training, recovery, school demands, nutrition, sleep, and stress.
4. Avoid attempting to correct falling performance with additional hard sessions.
5. Investigate specific symptoms and injuries.
Treatment and medicines

Management focuses on adequate recovery, correction of contributing problems, and an individualized rehabilitation plan.

No supplement, stimulant, or medication reliably cures overtraining syndrome. The duration of recovery varies, and prolonged cases may require substantial time.

Prevention and return

Coordinate workloads across teams and coaches. Monitor trends in well-being and performance without relying on one number.

Return should be gradual, with ongoing assessment of symptoms and function. A brief burst of improved performance does not necessarily demonstrate sustained recovery. pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov


J. Less common but important emergencies

1. Exertional heatstroke

What the condition is

Exertional heatstroke is life-threatening overheating associated with exercise and dysfunction of the brain or central nervous system. It can damage the kidneys, liver, heart, muscles, and other organs.

It may affect a fit athlete. Hot, humid conditions increase risk, but severe exertional overheating is not restricted to extreme weather.

Main causes and warning signs

High heat production during strenuous exercise can exceed the body’s ability to lose heat. Inadequate heat adaptation, excessive workload, equipment, illness, and other factors can contribute.

Confusion, unusual behavior, staggering, collapse, seizures, or reduced consciousness are key warning signs.

An athlete with exertional heatstroke may still be sweating heavily. Do not wait for dry skin.

Immediate first aid
1. Stop activity and activate emergency assistance.
2. Move the athlete from the heat.
3. Remove excess equipment and clothing.
4. Begin rapid cooling immediately.
5. A trained team should use whole-body cold-water immersion when available and safe, continuously supporting the head and airway.
6. If immersion is unavailable, wet the body with cool water, fan continuously, and use cold wet towels or packs while arranging transfer.
7. Continuously monitor breathing and responsiveness.
Do not delay cooling while searching for a thermometer. Do not leave an unconscious person unsupported in water. CDC

Professional treatment and medicines

The medical team manages core temperature, airway, circulation, and organ complications. Professional core-temperature monitoring guides treatment.

Paracetamol, ibuprofen, and aspirin do not treat heatstroke and should not be used to lower the temperature.

Prevention and return to sport

Use heat adaptation, environmental monitoring, workload modification, cooling breaks, and an emergency cooling plan.

Heatstroke requires hospital assessment and medically supervised recovery. There is no same-day return, and feeling better after cooling does not exclude organ injury. sccm.org
2. Severe exercise-associated hyponatremia

What the condition is

Exercise-associated hyponatremia means blood sodium has become abnormally low during or after exercise. Severe cases can cause brain swelling, seizures, breathing problems, and death.

It is often associated with drinking more fluid than the body can appropriately excrete. Exercise-related hormone responses can also contribute to water retention.

Warning signs

Headache, nausea, vomiting, bloating, confusion, poor coordination, seizures, or reduced consciousness may occur.

Symptoms can resemble heat illness or dehydration. A history of excessive drinking is important, but it does not establish the diagnosis.

Immediate first aid
1. Stop exercise and activate emergency medical care.
2. Check breathing and responsiveness.
3. Tell the medical team about fluid intake, exercise duration, symptoms, and medicines.
4. Do not force water, sports drinks, or other oral fluids.
5. Do not attempt treatment with salt tablets or concentrated homemade mixtures.
6. Monitor continuously and start CPR if indicated.
A confused athlete should receive nothing by mouth.

Professional treatment

Clinicians assess blood sodium and other possible causes of collapse. Severe symptomatic cases may require carefully administered hypertonic saline, with monitoring.

Ordinary saline is not automatically the correct treatment. Fluid management depends on the actual clinical situation.

Prevention and return to sport

Avoid forced overdrinking. Electrolyte-containing sports drinks do not prevent hyponatremia if consumed excessively.

A severe episode requires medical review and an individualized hydration and return-to-exercise plan. Do not return to competition after symptoms briefly improve. wms.org
3. Sudden cardiac arrest

What the condition is

Cardiac arrest occurs when the heart stops producing effective circulation. It differs from a heart attack, although a heart attack can cause arrest.

Without immediate treatment, the brain and other organs rapidly lose oxygen.

Warning signs

Sudden collapse, unresponsiveness, and absent or abnormal breathing are the main recognition signs.

Gasping is not normal breathing. Brief jerking or seizure-like activity can occur.

Immediate first aid
1. Activate emergency assistance and request the AED.
2. Begin chest compressions immediately when the person is unresponsive and not breathing normally.
3. For an adult-sized adolescent or adult, compress the center of the chest at 100–120 compressions per minute, approximately 5–6 cm deep, allowing full recoil.
4. Trained rescuers should provide breaths according to their CPR training.
5. If unable to give breaths, give continuous chest compressions rather than doing nothing.
6. Apply the AED and follow its spoken instructions.
7. Minimize interruptions and continue until professional help takes over or normal signs of life return.
For younger children, use pediatric CPR guidance and dispatcher instructions. Breaths are especially important in children and oxygen-related arrests. cpr.heart.org

Professional treatment and medicines

Emergency teams provide rhythm assessment, defibrillation, airway support, and medicines when indicated. Bystanders should not delay CPR to search for a pill or check glucose.

Prevention and return to sport

Have an accessible AED, trained responders, rehearsed roles, and a clear ambulance access route.

A survivor requires specialist investigation and an individualized participation decision. A normal appearance afterward does not establish safety. pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov
4. Dangerous heart rhythm disturbance

What the condition is

An arrhythmia is an abnormal heart rhythm. Some are harmless; others impair circulation or progress to cardiac arrest.

An athlete’s normal increase in heart rate during exercise is not itself an arrhythmia.

Causes and warning signs

Possible contributors include underlying electrical or structural heart disease, electrolyte problems, illness, and certain medicines or substances.

Palpitations accompanied by chest pain, breathlessness, dizziness, near-fainting, or fainting require urgent assessment.

Immediate first aid
1. Stop exercise.
2. Help the athlete rest safely.
3. Activate emergency assistance for current concerning symptoms.
4. Bring the AED and monitor responsiveness and breathing.
5. If collapse occurs with abnormal breathing, begin CPR and follow AED instructions.
6. Tell responders about medicines, supplements, and previous episodes.
Do not give someone else’s heart medicine or attempt untrained rhythm-conversion maneuvers.

Professional treatment

An ECG and clinical assessment guide treatment. Options may include medicines or electrical treatment, depending on the rhythm and the patient’s condition.

A pulse reading from a watch cannot reliably establish that a dangerous episode is harmless.

Prevention and return to sport

Report exertional fainting, recurrent palpitations, and relevant family history during medical assessment. Avoid stimulant misuse and disclose supplements.

Even if symptoms stop, exertional palpitations with faintness or chest pain need urgent review. Return requires diagnosis-specific clinical advice rather than a normal heart rate measured afterward. NHS
5. Severe asthma attack

What the condition is

A severe asthma attack causes substantial airway narrowing and breathing difficulty. It can progress to respiratory failure and cardiac arrest.

Warning signs

Difficulty speaking, marked breathing effort, exhaustion, blue or grey lips, drowsiness, or inadequate response to the prescribed reliever are concerning.

Very little wheeze can be a danger sign when airflow is severely reduced. Do not assume a quiet chest means improvement.

Immediate first aid
1. Stop activity and sit the athlete upright.
2. Activate emergency assistance immediately for severe symptoms.
3. Help them use their prescribed reliever according to their written asthma action plan.
4. Use a spacer with a compatible metered-dose inhaler when appropriate.
5. Keep them accompanied and monitor continuously.
6. Follow dispatcher instructions while waiting.
7. Start CPR if the athlete becomes unresponsive and is not breathing normally.
Do not make the athlete lie flat, breathe into a paper bag, or continue exercising.

Professional treatment and medicines

Treatment may include repeated inhaled bronchodilators, oxygen, systemic corticosteroids, and additional emergency therapies.

Reliever instructions differ between salbutamol regimens and suitable ICS–formoterol regimens. Do not assume that every inhaler can provide emergency relief.

Prevention and return to sport

Maintain appropriate preventive treatment, correct technique, an accessible reliever, and a written plan.

A severe attack needs medical assessment even if initial treatment helps. Arrange prompt follow-up and an individualized return plan; there is no automatic same-day clearance. nhs.uk
6. Anaphylaxis

What the condition is

Anaphylaxis is a severe, potentially life-threatening systemic allergic reaction. It may follow food, medicine, insect venom, latex, or another exposure.

In some people, exercise acts as a trigger or contributes alongside another exposure.

Warning signs

Throat or tongue swelling, hoarse voice, breathing difficulty, wheezing, faintness, collapse, or rapidly worsening symptoms may occur.

Hives can be present, but anaphylaxis can occur without an obvious rash.

Immediate first aid
1. Give the prescribed adrenaline—epinephrine—auto-injector immediately, following the device instructions and action plan.
2. Activate emergency medical assistance.
3. Have the person lie flat with legs raised when appropriate.
4. If breathing is difficult, allow a supported position that helps breathing without standing.
5. Do not allow standing or walking, even after apparent improvement.
6. If symptoms do not improve after approximately five minutes and a second prescribed device is available, give it according to the emergency plan.
7. Monitor continuously and begin CPR if required.
Treatment and medicines

Adrenaline is the first-line emergency treatment. Antihistamines and asthma inhalers do not replace it.

Hospital assessment is necessary because symptoms may persist or recur.

Prevention and return to sport

Identify triggers, maintain an allergy action plan, and ensure prescribed devices are accessible and in date.

A suspected episode requires emergency care and subsequent specialist review—not a return to competition after a rash fades. nhs.uk
7. Exertional rhabdomyolysis

What the condition is

Exertional rhabdomyolysis is significant muscle breakdown associated with exertion. Released muscle contents can damage the kidneys and disturb electrolytes, potentially affecting the heart.

Causes and symptoms

Unaccustomed intense exercise, excessive repeated effort, heat stress, and other medical or medication factors can contribute.

Warning signs include severe or unusual muscle pain, swelling, weakness, exercise intolerance, or tea- or cola-colored urine.

Dark urine is not required. Ordinary delayed-onset soreness and rhabdomyolysis cannot be reliably distinguished by appearance alone.

Immediate action
1. Stop activity.
2. Move out of the heat.
3. Obtain urgent medical assessment.
4. Monitor breathing, alertness, and urine-related information.
5. Do not attempt to treat a severely unwell athlete by forcing large volumes of water.
6. Report the recent workout, medicines, supplements, and associated heat symptoms.
Professional treatment

Blood and urine tests, kidney function, electrolytes, and sometimes ECG monitoring guide care.

Moderate or severe cases may need hospital admission and carefully monitored IV fluids. Complications can require additional treatment.

Painkillers, supplements, or massage do not reverse muscle breakdown. Avoid self-treatment that delays assessment.

Prevention and return to sport

Increase demanding exercise progressively, avoid punitive extreme workouts, and respond to unusual pain or weakness.

Return requires recovery and clinical review, often including laboratory follow-up and a graded activity plan. General statements about returning to work after mild cases must not be used as clearance for competitive sport. CDC
8. Serious traumatic brain injury

What the condition is

Serious traumatic brain injury can involve bleeding, swelling, skull fracture, or other structural damage. It differs from an uncomplicated concussion, although early symptoms may overlap.

Causes and warning signs

Falls, high-force collisions, or impacts from equipment can cause injury.

Worsening headache, repeated vomiting, increasing confusion, seizure, slurred speech, weakness, unequal pupils, double vision, or difficulty waking requires emergency assessment.

A person may initially appear relatively well before deteriorating.

Immediate first aid
1. Stop activity and activate emergency assistance.
2. Assess breathing and responsiveness.
3. Avoid unnecessary movement and consider associated neck injury.
4. Do not remove protective equipment unless necessary for lifesaving care and performed according to training.
5. Do not give food, drinks, sedatives, or alcohol.
6. Monitor continuously.
7. If vomiting threatens the airway, follow dispatcher instructions for safe positioning.
8. Begin CPR if breathing is absent or abnormal.
Do not restrain seizure movements or put anything in the mouth.

Professional treatment and medicines

Emergency teams assess airway, neurological status, and the need for imaging or neurosurgical treatment.

A painkiller making the headache less noticeable does not exclude dangerous injury. Do not delay transfer to observe whether symptoms settle.

Prevention and return to sport

Use appropriate equipment, safe facilities, and rule enforcement.

Serious head injury requires specialist management. No same-day return is appropriate, and eventual participation depends on the injury and medical advice. www.cdc.gov
9. Neck or spinal injury

What the injury is

A spinal injury may involve vertebrae, ligaments, discs, nerves, or the spinal cord. Movement can worsen some unstable injuries.

Causes and warning signs

Diving, falls, collisions, axial loading, or forceful neck movement may cause injury.

Neck or back pain, limb weakness, numbness, altered sensation, or loss of normal movement raises concern. Reduced consciousness or a distracting major injury can make assessment unreliable.

Immediate first aid
1. Activate emergency assistance.
2. Ask the athlete not to move.
3. Support the head in the position found without forcing alignment.
4. Do not sit the athlete up or test neck movement.
5. Do not remove helmets or other equipment casually.
6. Monitor breathing and responsiveness.
7. Follow dispatcher and trained-responder instructions.
Airway and breathing take priority. Necessary movement for CPR, airway protection, or escape from immediate danger should not be withheld because spinal injury is possible.

Do not perform traction or improvised spinal manipulation.

Professional treatment

Trained responders assess spinal protection, airway management, safe transfer, and imaging needs. Some injuries require surgery or specialist rehabilitation.

Prevention and return to sport

Enforce safe tackling, landing, and diving practices. Aquatic entry rules must reflect depth and conditions.

Return requires diagnosis-specific specialist advice. Temporary resolution of tingling or weakness does not establish that a spinal event was harmless. NICE
10. Acute compartment syndrome

What the condition is

Acute compartment syndrome occurs when pressure rises within a muscle compartment and threatens tissue circulation. Delayed treatment can cause permanent nerve and muscle damage.

It differs from chronic exertional compartment syndrome, which usually follows a recurring exercise-related pattern.

Causes and warning signs

Fractures, substantial muscle injury, bleeding, or excessive external compression can contribute.

Severe escalating pain, tense swelling, pain with gentle movement, tingling, or weakness are concerning.

A normal pulse does not exclude the condition. Late signs must not be awaited.

Immediate first aid
1. Stop activity and activate urgent emergency care.
2. Remove easily removable external compression, such as an overly tight wrap.
3. Do not attempt to cut off a cast yourself.
4. Support the limb around heart level rather than elevating it high above the heart.
5. Do not massage, stretch, or tightly rebandage it.
6. Do not delay transfer to see whether pain medicine helps.
Professional treatment

Urgent surgical assessment is essential. Acute compartment syndrome may require fasciotomy, an operation that releases pressure.

Pressure measurement may assist diagnosis in selected cases, but the assessment is time-critical.

Prevention and return to sport

Monitor significant injuries and ensure supports are not excessively tight. Worsening pain after an apparently stable injury requires reassessment.

Return follows surgical or specialist care and rehabilitation. It is not managed as an ordinary strain or bruise. NHS
11. Drowning

What the emergency is

Drowning is respiratory impairment caused by submersion or immersion in liquid. It can be fatal or nonfatal.

A person does not have to be completely underwater for a long period to experience serious oxygen deprivation.

Warning signs

A swimmer may be unable to call for help, struggle to keep the mouth clear, become unusually still, or lose consciousness. Drowning does not always involve dramatic splashing.

Immediate first aid
1. Alert the lifeguard and activate emergency assistance.
2. Avoid entering the water unless trained and able to rescue safely.
3. Use reaching or throwing assistance when appropriate.
4. Once safely removed, assess responsiveness and breathing.
5. If unresponsive and not breathing normally, start CPR.
6. Include rescue breaths when trained and able: oxygen deprivation is central to drowning.
7. Apply an AED promptly once available, without delaying initial CPR. Remove the person from standing water and dry the chest sufficiently for pads.
8. Continue according to training and dispatcher instructions.
If unable to provide breaths, chest compressions are better than no CPR.

Do not delay CPR to hang the person upside down or attempt to “drain water” with abdominal thrusts. ahajournals.org

Professional treatment

Responders may provide oxygen, ventilation, and hospital assessment. Anyone with breathing difficulty, persistent cough, abnormal behavior, or a need for resuscitation requires urgent evaluation.

Prevention and return to sport

Use qualified supervision, rescue equipment, safe depth rules, and rehearsed aquatic procedures.

After a drowning event, return requires medical advice. Apparent improvement does not automatically remove the need for assessment. American Heart Association
12. Severe hypoglycemia with confusion, seizure, or unconsciousness

What the condition is

Severe hypoglycemia is low blood glucose causing impairment that requires another person’s assistance. It can occur in athletes using insulin or certain other diabetes medicines.

Severity is determined by clinical impairment, not only a glucose number.

Causes and warning signs

Exercise, delayed food, and medication–activity mismatch can contribute. Confusion, unusual behavior, severe weakness, seizure, or unconsciousness may occur.

Exercise-related hypoglycemia can also develop after the event.

Immediate first aid
1. Stop activity and activate emergency assistance.
2. Check responsiveness and breathing.
3. If unresponsive and not breathing normally, begin CPR and use the AED.
4. Check glucose if equipment is available, without delaying lifesaving care.
5. Give the prescribed glucagon according to its instructions and the diabetes emergency plan.
6. If unconscious but breathing normally, protect the airway; use a recovery position when appropriate and when significant spinal injury is not suspected.
7. Monitor continuously.
Do not put food, drink, sugar, honey, or glucose gel into the mouth of someone unable to swallow safely.

Once fully alert and swallowing safely, follow the plan for fast-acting carbohydrate and subsequent food.

Professional treatment

Emergency clinicians may give IV glucose and investigate the cause. Glucagon response does not remove the need for monitoring, especially when recurrence is possible.

Prevention and return to sport

Maintain an individualized diabetes plan, glucose supplies, accessible glucagon, and trained support people.

A severe episode requires review and a safe plan before return. One improved reading is not automatic clearance. ADA
